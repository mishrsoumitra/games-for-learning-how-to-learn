Lecture 5 · The Desk and the Library · six instruments

How many things can you hold in your head at once?

Working memory is the space where you think, and it holds about four things at once. This lab measures yours six ways: how many digits you can hold, how many you can hold while adding one to each, how many chemistry characters you can hold when they spell formulas you already know, how many colours you can hold with no words to help you, how fast it empties when something else needs the space, and what happens to a list that keeps coming back. Before you start, write down your guess: how many digits can you hold?

Working memory · about four slots

Four things arrive, one at a time.

Six instruments

Instrument I · Digit span

Find the longest list you can hold.

The digit span task, modelled on the rule used in the Wechsler intelligence scales. Digits appear one per second. When they stop, type them back. If you get a length right the list grows by one; two misses at the same length ends the run. Your span is the longest list you got right.

Direction

Backward has to hold and work at the same time. It is usually one to two digits shorter.

How to run it

  1. Watch the digits. Do not write anything down and do not say them out loud.
  2. When Type them appears, enter the digits on the keypad (or your keyboard) and press Enter.
  3. In the backward half, type them in reverse: 4 8 2 becomes 2 8 4.
  4. The list grows by one every time you get it right. Everyone misses eventually. That is the measurement.

Instrument II · Digits, plus one

Hold the digits and change them at the same time.

This is Kahneman and Beatty's Add-1 task (1966). Digits arrive one per beat. After a two-beat pause you type them back with one added to each, one digit per beat, with no time to stop and think: 6 5 0 2 becomes 7 6 1 3, and 9 becomes 0. Then the same with three added. Holding and changing use the same space, and you will feel the strain.

Transform
Beat

You get two beats of grace at the end of each list. A list only counts if every digit is right and typed before the beats run out.

How to run it

  1. Digits appear on the beat. Hold them.
  2. A red +1 (or +3) appears for two beats. Then type the new digits, one per beat, on the keypad or your keyboard.
  3. Wrap past 9: with add one, 9 becomes 0; with add three, 8 becomes 1.
  4. Lists start at three digits and grow. When a length beats you twice, that half ends.

Instrument III · Formulas as chunks

The same characters, with and without meaning.

Characters from Class 9 to 12 chemistry appear one at a time: N, a, C, l. In the scrambled half they are in no order at all. In the formula half the same kinds of characters spell compounds you already know, run together with no gaps: NaClH2SO4CO2. You type back what you saw. This is the chess experiment of Chase and Simon (1973), with chemistry instead of chess and you as the expert.

Order
Pace

Capital and small letters matter while you watch (Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide), but your typing is checked without case, and a typed 0 counts as O.

How to run it

  1. Watch the characters. Say nothing, write nothing.
  2. When the box appears, type everything you saw, in order, and press Enter. Spaces are ignored.
  3. Scrambled lists start at four characters and grow by one. Formula lists start at two formulas and grow by one formula.
  4. After each list you see what it was. In the formula half, the compounds are named, so you can see which chunks you have and which you do not.

Instrument IV · Coloured squares

How many colours can you hold with no words?

The change-detection test of Luck and Vogel (1997). Two to eight coloured squares flash for a tenth of a second, the screen goes blank for just under a second, then one square comes back in its place. Same colour, or different? Luck and Vogel showed that the result does not change with a five-times-longer flash or with a spoken load, so this measures the visual store itself, not rehearsal. The estimate it gives, Cowan's K, is one of the main sources of the modern number of about four.

Length
Flash

A longer flash barely raises the number. That is the finding: the limit is in the store, not in the eye.

How to run it

  1. Look at the dot in the middle. The squares flash around it.
  2. When one square comes back inside a white ring, decide: is its colour the same as before, or different?
  3. Press S or ← for same, D or → for different, or tap the buttons.
  4. Half the trials change. Guess when you must. Guessing is part of how the number is worked out.
Running

Watch the squares. Keep your eyes on the dot.

Instrument V · Hold digits while counting

Hold four digits while you count backwards.

The Brown–Peterson task (Peterson and Peterson, 1959). Three consonants (or four digits) appear for two seconds. Then, for up to eighteen or twenty seconds, you count backwards in threes on the keypad, which keeps you from repeating the letters to yourself. Then you type the letters. In the original experiment, three-letter groups were nearly all gone after eighteen seconds. A control mode lets you just wait instead, to show that the counting, not the time, is what empties the space.

Delays
What you hold

Peterson and Peterson held letters and counted with numbers on purpose, so that the two interfere as little as possible.

During the delay

Counting is typed: from 482, you type 479, then 476. Each number submits itself after three digits. Keep to the beat, about one number every second or two. Speed matters more than accuracy. Keep going.

How to run it

  1. Three letters (or four digits) appear. Hold them.
  2. A three-digit number appears. Type the number three less, then three less again, until the screen changes. Keep a steady pace.
  3. Type the letters you were holding and press Enter.
  4. The delays come in random order. Nobody keeps everything at eighteen seconds. The curve is the result.

Instrument VI · A repeated list

Watch a chunk form in eight minutes.

Hebb's repetition experiment (1961). You see a run of digit lists, each a little longer than your span, and type back what you can. There is a pattern in the run that you are not told about. The results page reveals it, and what it did to your recall. It is the shortest demonstration we know of something moving from working memory into long-term memory.

List length

Lists

Lists are meant to be slightly too long. Getting six of nine is a normal result, not a failure.

How to run it

  1. Digits appear one per second. Watch them.
  2. Type back as many as you can, in their right positions. If you have lost a position, press · (or Space) to leave it blank and carry on.
  3. Press Enter when you are done. You are told how many you placed, not which.
  4. Do not hunt for a pattern. Just do each list as it comes.
