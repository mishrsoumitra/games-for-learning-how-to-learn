An experiment on memory, done on yourself

You see a few lines of algebra for five seconds. How much of it can you write again?

We are going to do a small experiment. A few lines of algebra come on the screen for five seconds, and then they are gone. You put back as many symbols as you can, line by line, by tapping them. You'll do this four times, and it takes about eight minutes. At the end, the page shows you how much you got back each time.

Do the experiment before you open the Guide. The Guide gives away what the experiment is looking for.

How to do it

  1. Keep your pen and paper away.
  2. Press Show the lines and look at them. Don't look away, five seconds is short.
  3. When the lines are gone, tap a symbol to put it on the line. Tap Next line to go to the line below. You can tap any line to go back to it.
  4. To take a symbol out, tap it and then tap Delete. To put a symbol in the middle, tap the symbol just before that place and then tap the new one.
  5. Put back only what you remember. A wrong symbol cancels a right one on the same line, so don't fill the lines with guesses.
  6. Press Done when you can't remember any more.

On a laptop you can also type. Use the letters, 2, =, +, -, / and the brackets. Press ^ for the small raised 2 and h for ½. Enter goes to the next line and the arrow keys move around.

How long you see the lines

Use 5 seconds unless your teacher says otherwise.

How many sets of lines

Nothing here is a test of how clever you are, or of how good your memory is. The experiment only shows what happens to our memory when we know the thing we are looking at. Everything stays in this browser unless you export it.

Guide · what the experiment is about and how this lab counts

How this lab works, and what the research says

Have you done the experiment? If not, go back and do it first. This page tells you what the experiment looks for, and once you know that, your result doesn't tell you anything about yourself.

What are we trying to see?

All of you have seen this in class. The teacher writes a derivation on the board. One student looks up once and copies three lines. Another student looks up and down ten times and copies it symbol by symbol. We usually say, "The first one has a very good memory."

This experiment checks whether that is true. You saw two kinds of lines. Both were made of the same symbols: v, u, a, t, the equals sign, the plus sign, the small raised 2. Both stayed on the screen for the same five seconds. In one kind, the symbols made a derivation from Class 11 physics. In the other kind, the same symbols were mixed up by chance.

If putting the lines back needed only a good memory, both kinds would be equally hard for you. Look at your result sheet. Were they?

The chess experiment that this lab copies

William Chase and Herbert Simon published this in 1973. They had three chess players: a master, a strong club player and a beginner. Each one looked at a position from the middle of a real game, with 24 to 26 pieces, for 5 seconds. Then the board was cleared and the player put back whatever they remembered.

  • The master put back about 16 pieces after one look. The club player put back about 8 and the beginner about 4.
  • Then they took the same pieces and put them on squares picked by chance. Now the master's advantage was gone. All three players did worse than the beginner had done on the real positions.
  • So the master did not have a better memory. The master had seen thousands of positions, and saw a castled king or a chain of pawns as one thing. Chase and Simon called each of these one chunk. The master's chunks had more pieces in them than the beginner's (about 2.5 against 1.9), and the master also put back more chunks on one board (7.7 against 5.3).

In this lab, the derivation is the real position and the mixed-up symbols are the pieces on chance squares. For a derivation we know, each of us is the master. For the mixed-up lines, each of us is the beginner.

Has this been done with algebra?

Yes, with single equations. Nobody we could find has done it with a full derivation of four or five lines, or with physics formulas. So the lines in this lab are our own version of the experiment.

School and university students in Sydney

John Sweller and Graham Cooper published this in 1985. They had 22 students of Year 9, 22 students of Year 11, and 18 university students who were training to teach Maths. Each student saw one line of algebra on a screen for 5 seconds and then had 30 seconds to write it down. Half of the lines were real equations, such as a + (ab/a) + ae/c = d. The other half were symbols in a random order, such as b = )dc (cb( )e + a.

  • For each line, they counted the longest run of symbols that the student wrote correctly without a break.
  • On the real equations, the Year 9 students got a run of about 8 symbols. The Year 11 students got about 10 and the university students about 11.
  • On the random lines, all three groups got about 4 and a half symbols. The older students were not better here.
  • The Year 11 students and the university students were not different from each other. Only the Year 9 students were lower.

The authors wrote that more experience in algebra went with a better memory for real equations, but not for random strings of symbols.

Class 9 students in the United States

David Geary and three others did the same thing in 2015 with 171 students of Class 9, and the same 5 seconds. Out of every 100 symbols, the students got back 44 to 55 of a real equation in one unbroken run, and 22 to 28 of a mixed-up one.

Do we see a real equation better, or do we hold it better?

Michael Ranney checked this in 1987. He showed calculus students a short line of algebra for about one tenth of a second and asked which symbol had been at one place. They were right 74 times out of 100 for real algebra and 74 times out of 100 for mixed-up symbols. So we don't see a real equation better at a glance. The difference comes in the seconds after that, when we have to hold it.

The same thing with circuit drawings

Dennis Egan and B. J. Schwartz did it in 1979 with electronics technicians and circuit drawings. After 5 or 10 seconds, six technicians put back more symbols of a real circuit drawing than six beginners did. When the same symbols were put at random places, the technicians were no better than the beginners. The authors also wrote that they could not rule out one thing: maybe the technicians were not remembering more, and were working out what must be there. Keep this in mind for your own result too.

How to compare your number with theirs. Compare loosely. Those students saw one equation at a time and wrote it with a pencil. You saw four or five lines together and tapped the symbols. Their count was the longest unbroken correct run, and this lab counts every right symbol and takes away the wrong ones.

How this lab makes the lines and counts your symbols

  • There are four derivations: the third equation of motion (v² = u² + 2as), the second equation of motion (s = ut + ½at²), work and kinetic energy, and kinetic energy from momentum (K = p²/2m). Each one has four or five lines and 32 to 47 symbols. They were written out for this lab.
  • For a mixed-up set, the lab takes every symbol of one derivation, mixes them by chance, and cuts them into lines of the same lengths as before. So a mixed-up set has exactly the same symbols and the same number of lines as a derivation.
  • In one run, each derivation is used only once, either as it is or mixed up. Which ones you get as they are is picked by chance.
  • To count, the lab puts each of your lines next to the line that was shown. A symbol is right when it is the right symbol and it comes in the right order on that line. If you miss one symbol in the middle, the symbols after it still count.
  • A symbol you put that was not there is wrong, and it cancels one right symbol on the same line. We tried filling every line with symbols picked by chance. Without this rule, that gets about 10 symbols right out of 40. With this rule, the lab counts about 2.
  • If you leave a line out and write the next one in its place, the lab still matches your lines with the right ones, as long as they are in the same order.
  • The number out of 100 is the symbols counted, divided by the symbols shown. This way of counting was made for this lab. It is not from a paper.

What to be careful about

  • On a derivation you know well, you did not only remember. You also worked it out again. You could have written most of it without seeing it at all. That is fine, and it is what the experiment is about, but it means your number is not a measurement of your memory.
  • The four derivations are not equally long, and you get two as they are and two mixed up. So one run is a small experiment. If the whole class does it, the numbers of the class together are worth much more than the number of one student.
  • Tapping symbols is slower than writing with a pen. While you look for a key, some of what you were holding goes away. This makes everybody's numbers a little lower, on both kinds of lines.
  • If you know a derivation by a different route, the lab still marks you against the lines that were shown, and you lose symbols.

What does this mean for how we study?

When a derivation looks impossible to remember, we often think, "I'm bad at remembering." The experiment says something else. Nobody can hold 40 separate symbols after one look, and nobody needs to. A derivation becomes easy to hold when we know its steps, because then there are only four or five things to hold.

  • Don't try to remember a derivation by reading it again and again. Close the book and do it by hand, from the first line to the last. Where you get stuck is the step you don't know yet.
  • After you finish a derivation, say its steps in words: "Start from v = u + at. Square both sides. Take 2a common. The bracket is s." If you can say the steps, you can write the symbols.
  • Look at what changes from one line to the next, and ask yourself why that was done. A line you can explain is one thing to hold. A line you can't explain is fifteen symbols to hold.
  • Chunks come from doing, many times, over many days. Do the same derivation again after two days and after a week, without looking.
