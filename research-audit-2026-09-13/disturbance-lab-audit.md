## A. Verified citations

Numbered so the tables below can refer to them.

**[1] Salamé, P., & Baddeley, A. D. (1982).** Disruption of short-term memory by unattended speech: Implications for the structure of working memory. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 21*(2), 150–164. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(82)90521-7 — *Snippet only* (CrossRef metadata and York repository record opened; neither carries the abstract). Abstract as excerpted: a series of experiments on immediate memory for visually presented digits; unattended speech impaired recall; impairment increased with the phonological similarity of the irrelevant words to the digits; semantic content of the speech did not matter; articulatory suppression removed the effect. Establishes the phonological-similarity result and the suppression-abolishes-it result. Presentation rate, dB level and any intrusion counts could not be checked.

**[2] Salamé, P., & Baddeley, A. D. (1987).** Noise, unattended speech and short-term memory. *Ergonomics, 30*(8), 1185–1194. https://doi.org/10.1080/00140138708966007 (PMID 3691472). — *Snippet only* (PubMed and CrossRef opened; both lack the abstract). Abstract as excerpted from the T&F page: "Three experiments required the immediate serial recall of sequences of nine visually presented digits accompanied by silence, noise or unattended speech in an unfamiliar language. Experiments 1 and 2 showed a clear effect of unattended speech at both 75 dB(A) and 95 dB(A), while unattended noise had no effect in either study. Experiment 3 used a separate groups design combining 95 dB(A) noise and quiet with instructions either to remain silent or to rehearse overtly." Corroborated by [3], which I did open in full and which lists Salamé & Baddeley 1983 and 1987 among studies that "observe no effect of noise on retention".

**[3] Salamé, P., & Baddeley, A. (1989).** Effects of background music on phonological short-term memory. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 41A*(1), 107–122. https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748908402355 — *Full text opened* (https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/1989-salame.pdf). Method: nine visually presented digits at 1 per 750 ms, 2-s warning, written serial recall in 13 s, subjects told to group in threes and stay silent; silence = 37 dB(A) ambient; music/speech/noise = 75 dB(A) over loudspeakers. Exp 1 (n = 24, mean age 19): mean % errors — silence 36.6, instrumental 41.4, vocal 50.8 (Table 1), all pairwise p < .01. Exp 2 (practised subjects): vocal > instrumental; instrumental not significantly worse than silence. Exp 3 (n = 24): quiet vs Arabic prose vs instrumental music vs *pink noise amplitude-modulated by the envelope of the Arabic speech*; noise = silence < instrumental < speech (Newman–Keuls, p < .01). Establishes: speech > instrumental music > amplitude-modulated noise = silence; baseline error rate for 9 visual digits.

**[4] Colle, H. A., & Welsh, A. (1976).** Acoustic masking in primary memory. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 15*(1), 17–31. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(76)90003-7 — *Abstract opened* (ERIC EJ138216 via the ERIC API: "Two experiments … concurrent auditory stimulation should destroy memory information and thus reduce recall performance"). Design details from [31] (opened): eight consonants presented visually one after another, written serial recall, a foreign language over earphones, subjects told to ignore it; Exp I: recall of phonologically dissimilar lists reduced, similar lists not; about a 12% decrease. The language is described as "foreign"/"unfamiliar" in every source I could open; I could not confirm "German".

**[5] Jones, D. M., Madden, C., & Miles, C. (1992).** Privileged access by irrelevant speech to short-term memory: The role of changing state. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 44A*(4), 645–669. https://doi.org/10.1080/14640749208401304 (PMID 1615168). — *Abstract opened* (PubMed). Exp 1: simple unvarying or repetitive speech sounds do not disrupt; Exp 2: speech analogues with regular or irregular amplitude envelopes on a range of carriers fail to imitate speech; Exp 3: between-utterance phonological variability is crucial; Exp 4: predictability of the syllable sequence does not reduce disruption.

**[6] Jones, D. M., & Macken, W. J. (1993).** Irrelevant tones produce an irrelevant speech effect: Implications for phonological coding in working memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 19*(2), 369–381. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.19.2.369 — *Snippet only* (CrossRef metadata opened). Abstract as excerpted: a stream of changing tones is as disruptive of visual serial recall as four syllables (Exps 1–2); a repeated syllable that changes only in pitch also disrupts (Exp 3); the effect arises during storage, not encoding. Secondary confirmation from [33] (opened): "repeated tones or syllables (i.e., 'steady-state sound') do not produce disruption compared to silence".

**[7] Jones, D. M., Macken, W. J., & Murray, A. C. (1993).** Disruption of visual short-term memory by changing-state auditory stimuli: The role of segmentation. *Memory & Cognition, 21*(3), 318–328. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03208264 (PMID 8316094). — *Abstract opened*. Continuous random pitch glides do not disrupt; glides interrupted by silence do; a physically continuous stream of *synthesized vowel sounds* does disrupt.

**[8] Beaman, C. P., & Jones, D. M. (1997).** Role of serial order in the irrelevant speech effect: Tests of the changing-state hypothesis. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 23*(2), 459–471. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.23.2.459 — *Snippet only* (CrossRef metadata opened; ORCA, academia.edu, ResearchGate and Semantic Scholar PDF all blocked). Abstract as excerpted: effects of irrelevant speech examined across a range of memory tasks; a *missing-item* task, which relies on a non-serial strategy, is less sensitive than a task calling on memory for serial order; tasks involving memory for serial order are particularly susceptible. The abstract text I could see does not mention free recall.

**[9] Beaman, C. P., & Jones, D. M. (1998).** Irrelevant sound disrupts order information in free recall as in serial recall. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 51A*(3), 615–636. https://doi.org/10.1080/713755774 (PMID 9745380). — *Abstract opened*. Irrelevant sound disrupted order and item information equally under serial and free recall instructions (Exp 1); an effect on free recall of particularly long lists was also shown (Exp 2).

**[10] Salamé, P., & Baddeley, A. (1990).** The effects of irrelevant speech on immediate free recall. *Bulletin of the Psychonomic Society, 28*(6), 540–542. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03334073 — *Snippet only* (Springer served HTML instead of the PDF). Free recall of 16 visually presented words: no impairment under speech relative to control.

**[11] Ellermeier, W., & Hellbrück, J. (1998).** Is level irrelevant in "irrelevant speech"? Effects of loudness, signal-to-noise ratio, and binaural unmasking. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 24*(5), 1406–1414. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.24.5.1406 (PMID 9988598). — *Abstract opened*. Exp 1: the detrimental effect of irrelevant sound (speech or music) on serial recall was the same at 75 dB(A) and 60 dB(A). Exp 2: adding pink noise to the speech gave a linear improvement in performance with decreasing speech-to-noise ratio. Exp 3: binaural unmasking negligible.

**[12] Ellermeier, W., & Zimmer, K. (1997).** Individual differences in susceptibility to the "irrelevant speech effect". *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 102*(4), 2191–2199. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.419596 (PMID 9348677). — *Abstract opened*. 72 subjects; visually presented digits; foreign speech, pink noise and silence mixed trial by trial. Individual speech-minus-silence effects "normally distributed over a wide range extending from slight facilitation to severe disruption"; 25 subjects retested after four weeks, r_tt = 0.45; internal consistency α = 0.55; raw error rates per condition reliable at ≈0.85; "Subjects were quite inaccurate in assessing their individual impairment"; questionnaire noise sensitivity explained little variance.

**[13] Banbury, S. P., Macken, W. J., Tremblay, S., & Jones, D. M. (2001).** Auditory distraction and short-term memory: Phenomena and practical implications. *Human Factors, 43*(1), 12–29. https://doi.org/10.1518/001872001775992462 (PMID 11474757). — *Abstract opened* (PubMed and CrossRef). The abstract contains no percentage and no habituation figure; it says only that "relatively quiet background sound will have a marked effect". Full text not obtainable (SAGE, ResearchGate, Gale all 403).

**[14] Beaman, C. P. (2004).** The irrelevant sound phenomenon revisited: What role for working memory capacity? *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 30*(5), 1106–1118. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.30.5.1106 (PMID 15355139). — *Abstract opened*; the sentence "a decrement in recall performance usually estimated at approximately 30% to 50%" is attributed to this paper by a search-engine excerpt (the Reading-hosted PDF returned 403). Abstract: no evidence that high-OSPAN people resist irrelevant speech in serial or free recall; low-OSPAN participants made more semantically related *intrusion errors from the irrelevant stream* in free recall (Exp 4).

**[15] Baddeley, A. D., Lewis, V., & Vallar, G. (1984).** Exploring the articulatory loop. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 36A*(2), 233–252. https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748408402157 — *Abstract opened* (CrossRef deposit): "A series of five experiments explore the influence of articulatory suppression on immediate memory for **auditorily presented** items … Experiments 1, 2 and 3 demonstrate that the phonological similarity effect is not abolished by articulatory suppression … Experiments 4 and 5 show that the [word-length effect] is abolished by articulatory suppression, even when presentation is auditory, provided suppression occurs during both input and recall." Nothing about digit span under visual presentation.

**[16] Chincotta, D., & Underwood, G. (1997).** Digit span and articulatory suppression: A cross-linguistic comparison. *European Journal of Cognitive Psychology, 9*(1), 89–96. — *Full text opened* (http://jtoomim.org/brain-training/digit%20span%20and%20articulatory%20suppression.pdf). Digits shown one at a time on a screen (English group: 1 per 1.5 s); suppression = saying "la-la" at two phrases per second from 2 s before the list until recall; span = last length recalled correctly (two lists per length, stop after two failures). Across six languages: control span 7.31 vs suppressed 5.78 (p < .01); English ≈ 7.5 → 5.2 (from Fig. 1, as read by [36]).

**[17] Baddeley, A. D., Thomson, N., & Buchanan, M. (1975).** Word length and the structure of short-term memory. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 14*, 575–589. — *Full text opened* (https://labs.la.utexas.edu/gilden/files/2016/03/1-s2.0-S0022537175800454-main.pdf). Exp VII: suppression = "subjects counted repeatedly from one to eight, keeping rate of articulation as constant as possible at about three digits per second", from before the list until recall; lists at 1.5 s per item. Exp VIII: visual vs auditory presentation at 2 s per item; suppression × modality interaction, F(1,15) = 33.13; "suppression has been shown to have a large effect on visually presented material, but little effect on auditorily presented material" (citing Levy 1971; Peterson & Johnson 1971). Attributes the suppression technique to Murray (1968).

**[18] Murray, D. J. (1968).** Articulation and acoustic confusability in short-term memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology, 78*(4, Pt 1), 679–684. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0026641 — *Not opened* (metadata only). Origin of the "repeat 'the'" suppression procedure with visually presented letters (per [17] and secondary sources).

**[19] Hughes, R. W. (2014).** Auditory distraction: A duplex-mechanism account. *PsyCh Journal, 3*(1), 30–41. https://doi.org/10.1002/pchj.44 (PMID 26271638). — *Abstract opened*. Interference-by-process (changing-state) vs attentional capture (deviants); capture is controllable by task engagement, interference-by-process is not.

**[20] Hughes, R. W., Vachon, F., & Jones, D. M. (2007).** Disruption of short-term memory by changing and deviant sounds. *JEP: LMC, 33*(6), 1050–1061. (PMID 17983312). — *Abstract opened*. Changing-state and deviation effects are functionally distinct.

**[21] Hughes, R. W., & Jones, D. M. (2005).** The impact of order incongruence between a task-irrelevant auditory sequence and a task-relevant visual sequence. *JEP: HPP, 31*(2), 316–327. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.31.2.316 (PMID 15826233). — *Abstract opened*. Serial recall of visual digits is disrupted **more** by an irrelevant auditory sequence of the *same digits* than by irrelevant consonants, but only when the spoken order is incongruent with the list; disruption scales with the number of order-incongruent transitions.

**[22] Tremblay, S., Nicholls, A. P., Alford, D., & Jones, D. M. (2000).** The irrelevant sound effect: Does speech play a special role? *JEP: LMC, 26*(6), 1750–1754. (PMID 11185795). — *Abstract opened*. Sine-wave speech disrupts equally whether heard as speech or not, and less than natural speech.

**[23] LeCompte, D. C., Neely, C. B., & Wilson, J. R. (1997).** Irrelevant speech and irrelevant tones. *JEP: LMC, 23*(2), 472–483. (PMID 9080015). — *Abstract opened*. Meaningful speech > meaningless speech > tones in disruption (Exps 1–4).

**[24] Macken, W. J., & Jones, D. M. (1995).** Functional characteristics of the inner voice and the inner ear: Single or double agency? *JEP: LMC, 21*(2), 436–448. (PMID 7738509). — *Abstract opened*. Suppression during retention of 9-item lists shows a changing-state effect; vocalised suppression more disruptive than mouthed; the effect occurs only with tests of serial order.

**[25] Larsen, J. D., & Baddeley, A. (2003).** Disruption of verbal STM by irrelevant speech, articulatory suppression, and manual tapping. *QJEP, 56A*(8), 1249–1268. (PMID 14578082). — *Abstract opened*. Suppression has a major impact and removes the phonological similarity effect; irrelevant speech (six tokens) impairs but leaves the similarity effect (Exp 3).

**[26] Tremblay, S., & Jones, D. M. (1998).** Role of habituation in the irrelevant sound effect: Evidence from the effects of token set size and rate of transition. *JEP: LMC, 24*(3), 659–671. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.24.3.659 — *Not opened* (CrossRef metadata; findings from secondary descriptions: the largest rise in disruption is from one to two tokens; results read as favouring changing state over habituation).

**[27] Banbury, S., & Berry, D. C. (1997).** Habituation and dishabituation to speech and office noise. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 3*(3), 181–195. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.3.3.181 — *Snippet only* (CentAUR blocked). Memory-for-prose tasks; background speech was habituated to after 20 min exposure; office noise without speech also; 5 min of quiet partially restored the disruption.

**[28] Bell, R., Röer, J. P., Dentale, S., & Buchner, A. (2012).** Habituation of the irrelevant sound effect: Evidence for an attentional theory of short-term memory disruption. *JEP: LMC, 38*(6), 1542–1557. (PMID 22612161). — *Abstract opened*. ISE abolished after 20 min of passive listening to the distractor speech (Exp 1); reduced after 45 s (Exps 2–4); only when the material matches (Exp 5).

**[29] Röer, J. P., Bell, R., & Buchner, A. (2014).** Evidence for habituation of the irrelevant-sound effect on serial recall. *Memory & Cognition, 42*(4), 609–621. (PMID 24203781). — *Abstract opened*. Disruption by to-be-ignored speech and music "markedly reduced after eight repetitions", blocked or random order, also when sound is confined to the retention interval.

**[30] Röer, J. P., Rummel, J., Bell, R., & Buchner, A. (2017).** Metacognition in auditory distraction. *Journal of Cognition, 1*(1), 2. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6645164/ — *Full text opened*. Eight digits at 1 Hz (800 ms on, 200 ms off), sound at 65 dB(A) over headphones; expectations about distractibility changed subjective ratings but not the objective effect.

**[31] Leist, L., Lachmann, T., & Klatte, M. (2023).** Irrelevant speech impairs serial recall of verbal but not spatial items in children and adults. *Memory & Cognition* (PMC9950248). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9950248/ — *Full text opened*. "The task used in these studies requires immediate serial recall of sequences of five to nine unrelated items (e.g., digits, letters, words) presented one-by-one on a screen, with a rate of one to two items per second" (citing Colle 1980; [11]). "Steady-state sounds, for example, babble noise or repetitions of single syllables or tones, have little or no effect." Adults: 8 items at 1,500 ms + 500 ms ISI; natural recorded syllables at 62 dB LAeq; proportion correct silence .70, steady-state .70, changing-state significantly lower (p < .001).

**[32] Leist, L., Lachmann, T., & Klatte, M. (2025).** Impact of irrelevant speech and non-speech sounds on serial recall. *Scientific Reports* (PMC11732993). — *Full text opened*. "Immediate serial recall of lists of 5 to 9 items presented in succession for about one second each"; environmental sounds impaired verbal recall slightly (p = .019), speech much more (p < .001).

**[33] Kattner, F., Hanl, S., Paul, L., & Ellermeier, W. (2023).** Task-specific auditory distraction in serial recall and mental arithmetic. *Memory & Cognition, 51*(4), 930–951 (PMC9569014). — *Full text opened*. Eight digits (1–9 without replacement) at 2 s per digit; natural recorded consonant names; steady-state ≈ .70 vs changing-state ≈ .63 proportion correct, F(1,30) = 27.28.

**[34] Kattner, F., & Meinhardt, R. (2020).** Dissociating the disruptive effects of irrelevant music and speech on serial recall of tonal and verbal sequences. *Frontiers in Psychology, 11*:346 (PMC7066211). — *Full text opened*. States "white noise (which was shown to produce no disruption of serial recall compared to silence" citing Salamé & Baddeley 1989; Jones, Miles & Page 1990; [12]; [11]. Their own verbal recall: white noise .761 vs speech .738.

**[35] Woods, D. L., et al. (2011).** Improving digit span assessment of short-term verbal memory. *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology, 33*(1), 101–111 (PMC2978794). — *Full text opened*. Auditory digits at 1/s; "mean span" = list length at which 50% of lists are correct; young adults (n = 30, mean age 26): forward MS 7.41 (SD 0.94); broad adult sample (n = 763, 18–65): 6.52 (SD 1.00).

**[36] Mattys, S. L., Baddeley, A., & Trenkic, D. (2018).** Is the superior verbal memory span of Mandarin speakers due to faster rehearsal? *Memory & Cognition, 46*(3), 361–369 (PMC5880866). — *Full text opened*. "Although articulatory suppression reduces digit span by around two items, it does not totally disrupt performance. For example, Chincotta and Underwood (1997) found a reduction in digit span under suppression from around 8.9 to 6.2 digits … for Chinese participants and from 7.5 to 5.2 for English participants."

**[37] Schlittmeier, S. J., Hellbrück, J., Thaden, R., & Vorländer, M. (2008).** The impact of background speech varying in intelligibility. *Ergonomics, 51*(5), 719–736. (PMID 18432448). — *Abstract opened*. Speech at 35 dB(A) with poor intelligibility no longer disrupted verbal STM relative to 55 dB(A) intelligible speech; soft but intelligible speech still did.

## C. Design audit, round by round

### C0. Baseline numbers to judge the design against
- Standard ISE procedure: 5–9 items, one at a time on screen, 1–2 items per second, written or typed serial recall; sound during presentation and/or retention ([31], [32]). Specific examples: 9 digits at 1/750 ms with 13 s recall [3]; 8 digits at 1 Hz (800/200 ms) [30]; 8 digits at 2 s [33]; 8 pictures at 1.5 s + 0.5 s [31].
- Sound levels used: 60–75 dB(A) [11], 65 dB(A) [30], 62 dB LAeq [31], 75 dB(A) [3], 75/95 dB(A) [2]; silence ≈ 37 dB(A) ambient [3].
- Baseline accuracy: 9 visual digits at 1/750 ms in silence gave 36.6% errors in first-year students [3], i.e. about 5.7 of 9 in place. Young adults' auditory span (50% of lists perfect) is 7.4 digits [35]. So for a 7-digit list in silence a typical student should place roughly 5.5–6 of 7 correctly and get a bit over half the lists perfect.
- Size of the effect: "30–50%" more errors is the figure in circulation ([14], snippet); concrete data are +14 percentage points of errors for vocal music on 9 digits [3], ≈12% for foreign speech on 8 consonants [4 via 31], 7 percentage points for changing- vs steady-state speech on 8 digits [33]. On 7 digits that is roughly **0.5–1 digit per list**, with individual effects spanning zero to large [12].

### C1. Round 1: Silence (baseline)
- Procedure: 7 digits, whole list on screen for N × 0.7 s (4.9 s), then typed serial recall; or one at a time at 700 ms on / 300 ms off.
- Match to paradigm: the "one at a time, one per second" option matches the standard (1/s, cf. [30], [31]). **The default whole-list 5-second display has no counterpart in any ISE study I found**; every study presents items sequentially. Sequential presentation forces serial encoding and rehearsal, which is what the changing-state stream interferes with ([8], [21]). A 5-s simultaneous display invites visual grouping/chunking and reading strategies; [3] notes that when subjects stop using a phonological code the speech effect shrinks. Whether the effect survives simultaneous presentation is therefore **unverified**, not contradicted.
- List construction: game allows a digit up to twice and bans runs of three. Lab lists usually sample without replacement ([33]: 1–9 without replacement; [16]: no contiguous identical items, no ascending/descending runs). Repeated digits make "right digit, wrong place" ambiguous and inflate position-correct by chance.
- Recommendations: (1) make "one at a time" the default for solo runs and keep the 5-s board for the projector only; (2) draw 7 distinct digits (from 0–9 or 1–9) with no runs of three; (3) keep 2–3 lists per round, ideally 3 (the reading already says 1 list is a coin toss, correctly).

### C2. Round 2: Loud, steady white noise
- Procedure: looped 2-s white-noise buffer, gain from a 10–100 slider (default 70), from countdown until Enter.
- Match: broadband noise as the "steady" control is exactly the lab control ([2], [11] pink noise, [12] pink noise, [3] amplitude-modulated pink noise). Continuous noise through recall matches [3] ("during presentation and recall"). A 2-s loop is fine; any audible loop click would be a "change" and should be checked.
- Level: unknown in dB; the slider is a gain. Lab levels were 60–95 dB(A). The instruction "turned up until it is annoying" is defensible but uncalibrated; at very high phone volumes it may be louder than anything tested. Recommend a note: "as loud as a busy road, not painful".
- Note the game's claim "steady noise costs nothing at any loudness" is only tested to 95 dB(A) (B31).

### C3. Round 3: Quiet, changing voice (TTS digits)
- Procedure: Web Speech voice says random digits, no immediate repeats, ~1 per 950 ms, pitch jittered ±8%, from countdown until Enter; fallback: 9 triangle tones, 260 ms, random 550–950 ms gaps.
- Match to paradigm: a token stream at ~1/s of changing items is the canonical changing-state stream ([5], [6], [30]); ten tokens is more than enough ([26]: most of the effect appears at two tokens). Sound through presentation, retention and recall matches [3] and is at least as strong as retention-only ([29] Exp 4).
- Synthetic voice: no study I opened used consumer TTS. Synthesised vowel streams disrupt [7]; sine-wave speech disrupts, but less than natural speech [22]; tones disrupt, less than speech [23]. Expect a modern TTS voice to work; the size relative to a natural voice is **unverified**.
- Digits as the irrelevant stream: this is a deliberate content match. [21] shows that the same set of digits in an order incongruent with the list disrupts **more** than irrelevant consonants, and [1] shows phonologically similar irrelevant items disrupt more. So the round probably produces a larger effect than foreign prose, which is good for the demonstration but blurs the "it is only the changing that matters" message; the guide should say the voice was chosen to maximise the effect. A "voice you cannot understand" round (README's future item 2) is the clean test.
- The tone fallback is well founded ([6], [7]: segmented, pitch-changing tones).
- Intrusion scoring: a typed digit not on the board counts as "said by the voice" if the voice said it anywhere between 2 s before the board and Enter. The voice speaks ~1 digit/s for the whole 15–25 s window with no immediate repeats, so it has almost certainly said every digit 0–9; **practically every foreign digit will be labelled an intrusion by chance**. The metric cannot distinguish "wrote what I heard" from a random slip. Fix: either (a) narrow the window (e.g. the digit was spoken within ~2 s before that keystroke, or during the board), and compare the rate with the same "foreign digit" rate in the silence and noise rounds; or (b) rename it "digits not on the board" and drop the causal claim. This also matters because the literature gives no intrusion rate to compare against (B11).
- Order-error share: errs = order + foreign + missing in disturbed rounds; orderShare = order / errs. Reasonable, but with repeated digits allowed (C1) a repeated digit typed once in the wrong place is counted as an order error when it may be an omission; drawing distinct digits fixes this.

### C4. Round 4: Busy mouth (articulatory suppression)
- Procedure: say the table of 3/5/7 aloud, paced by a visual pulse at 1/s, from countdown until Enter.
- Match: the lab task is a low-content, fast, continuous utterance: "the, the, the" [18], counting 1–8 at ≈3 per second [17], "la-la" at 2 phrases per second [16]; always through input and, for the strongest effect, through recall too ([15] on the word-length effect; [16] until the RECALL prompt). The game's pacing (≈1 item/s) is **two to three times slower** than the lab, and a times table is a generative arithmetic sequence rather than a rote repetition, which adds a general-attention load the lab task avoids (and, per [24], vocalised suppression that itself changes state is more disruptive, so a table may over-shoot for the wrong reason).
- Expected size: ≈2 digits of span with visual digits [16]; the reading's "about two digits of span" is right, but the citation must change (B13).
- Recommendations: offer "say 'the-the-the' (or 'one-two-three-four') as fast as you comfortably can, about twice a second" as the default, keep the times table as the classroom/fun option; keep it running until Enter; state in the guide that lab pacing is 2–3 syllables per second.
- Presentation: with a 5-s whole-list board, suppression may hurt less than in the lab because visual/spatial encoding is available; the one-at-a-time option is again the cleaner form.

### C5. Scoring and verdict bands
- Digits-in-place is the standard measure (B26). Cost = silence mean minus round mean, per list. With 2 lists of 7, the per-round mean has a standard error of roughly 0.5–0.8 digits; the expected voice cost is ≈0.5–1 digit (C0). The "Textbook" band requires cost ≥ 1 digit (≥ 1.5 for ten), so on the literature's own numbers **most honest runs will land in "Unshaken"** unless the person is at or above span. Options: lower T to 0.5 digit when ≥ 3 lists were run; or express the cost as percent of silence errors (the literature's unit) and call anything ≥ 30% "textbook"; or show the cost with a ± band.
- "Both rooms hurt" reading says a drop under noise "is usually noise in the measurement" — consistent with [12] (individual noise effects scatter around zero) but say "usually" rather than implying it cannot happen.
- Score out of 100 (percent in place across all lists) is fine as a trend number; note it will drop when more disturbed rounds are included, so compare only same-round-set runs (the guide already says "same settings").

### C6. Projector mode
- 20 s to write, then reveal for self-scoring by show of hands: matches paper-and-pencil serial recall ([3] used 13 s). One list per round is a demonstration, not a measurement, as the README says.
- The sound plays through room speakers at an unknown level; with a TTS voice at "talking volume" in a large room, level will vary by seat; the guide could say this.

## D. Statements no source supports, to remove or soften

1. "Most people lose one to three of seven to the voice; almost nobody loses anything to the noise." (reading, Unshaken band) — too large by roughly a factor of two; see C0. Replace with "about half a digit to one digit of seven per list on average, and nothing on average under noise".
2. "In the laboratory studies the irrelevant items rarely appear in the answer" / "That is the usual laboratory result: the voice rarely puts its own words on your page." (README, Guide, reading) — no intrusion rate found in any source; the lab papers do not, as far as I could check, report one. Say instead that the studies count digits in position and predict order errors.
3. "usually as a picture of the board" (Guide round 4, reading) — speculation; drop.
4. "Habituation is weak: people do not stop being disrupted by the same kind of sound over sessions (Banbury et al., 2001)." — contradicted for a *repeated identical* recording by [27], [28], [29]; the claim is defensible only for *new* speech. Rewrite.
5. "Music that stays the same … is closer to the fan." — no study; mark as a prediction from the changing-state rule.
6. "rain, traffic hum" as inert — not tested in the cited work; environmental sounds gave a small impairment in [32]. Keep "fan, air-conditioner, white noise".
7. "Loudness … is irrelevant" without a range — bound it to 60–95 dB(A) and note [37].
8. "nearly everyone is affected" and "fairly stable" (Ellermeier & Zimmer) — the paper reports a normal distribution reaching into facilitation and r = 0.45; say "most people, to very different degrees, and only moderately stable".
9. "vocal music disrupts about as much as speech" — not compared directly in [3].
10. "tones … disrupt in the same way" — they disrupt less [23], [22].
11. "Colle and Welsh (1976) … German" — unverified; say "a foreign language".
12. Attribution of "about 30%" / "a third to a half" to Banbury et al. 2001 — unverified from the abstract; the phrase is locatable in Beaman 2004 (snippet).
13. "Baddeley, Lewis & Vallar (1984) … with visual presentation … about two digits" — wrong paper (auditory presentation); use [16].
14. "steady noise costs nothing at any loudness" — say "up to 95 dB(A), the loudest tested".
15. Guide round 3: "This page checks every wrong digit against what the voice said in that window" — technically true but, given C3, the check is nearly always positive; either fix the window or stop presenting it as a test of the slide's prediction.
