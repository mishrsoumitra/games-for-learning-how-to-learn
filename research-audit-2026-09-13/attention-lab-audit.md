# Attention Lab — research audit (13 September 2026)

Scope: `attention-lab.html` (Guide text, per-instrument constants, `Readings`, `Score`) and the README section "## Attention Lab" ("The research behind it" and the score tables). Every claim below was checked against a source I actually opened (PMC/Europe PMC abstracts and full texts, Frontiers full texts, author PDFs, Crossref/Semantic Scholar metadata). Where a primary paper could not be opened (paywalled or 403) the claim is marked **UNVERIFIED** and the best secondary source I did open is named. Nothing here was taken from Wikipedia alone.

How the game's numbers were read: constants `CK_RANGES`, `SKIP_WINDOW = 2500`, `SART = {stim: 250, soa: 1150, sizes: [48,72,94,100,120], practice: 18}`, `PvtTest.settings()` (`isi: 3 min → [1000,4000] else [2000,10000]`, `lapseMs: 3 min → 355 else 500`, false start = press during wait or RT < 100 ms, feedback 900 ms), `MRT = {beat: 1300, tone: 75, window: 650}` with probes every 23–46 beats, `Overlay.probe()` options, `countVerdict()` (tracking ≤ 5 % or ≤ 3 s; slipping ≤ 15 %), `Readings.*` strings, `Score.*` interpolation tables, and the Guide `<section>`s.

## A. Verified citations

Each entry: full reference · URL actually opened · what it establishes.

**A1. Mackworth, N. H. (1948).** The breakdown of vigilance during prolonged visual search. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1*(1), 6–21. doi:10.1080/17470214808416738. Metadata only (SAGE/T&F page returned 403; Semantic Scholar record confirms title/volume/pages). Primary text **not opened**. Secondary sources opened: Ustun et al. (2019) arXiv:1912.04823 (quotes Mackworth's signal schedule: 12 signals per 30 min at intervals 0.75, 0.75, 1.5, 2, 2, 1, 5, 1, 1, 2, 3 min, no signals in the last 10 min of each half hour, response by Morse key); Klein & Feltmate (2025) *Frontiers in Cognition 4*:1632885 (Fig. 1: "signal rates of about 30/hour"; decrement robust "after the first half hour"; signal = "double-step"); Neurobs description page and Wikipedia (decrement of 10–15 % in the first half hour; 2-h watch; pointer in short jumps about once a second; designed to simulate radar watch).

**A2. Lichstein, K. L., Riedel, B. W., & Richman, S. L. (2000).** The Mackworth Clock Test: A computerized version. *The Journal of Psychology, 134*(2), 153–161. doi:10.1080/00223980009600858. Opened: Crossref record, Semantic Scholar record, ProQuest preview (no abstract text available). Establishes only the bibliographic fact. **Note: the journal is *The Journal of Psychology*, not *Perceptual and Motor Skills*.** Parameters (positions, step, signal) **not verified**; the Inquisit manual (millisecond.com, opened) describes its own script "heavily inspired by Lichstein et al." as 100 slots, 1-s dwell, skip signal, 12 skips per 30-min round — that is Inquisit's implementation, not a quotation of Lichstein.

**A3. Robertson, I. H., Manly, T., Andrade, J., Baddeley, B. T., & Yiend, J. (1997).** 'Oops!': Performance correlates of everyday attentional failures in traumatic brain injured and normal subjects. *Neuropsychologia, 35*(6), 747–758. doi:10.1016/S0028-3932(97)00015-8. Opened: Europe PMC abstract (34 TBI, 75 controls; targets 1 in 9; "errors … can be predicted by a significant shortening of reaction times in the immediately preceding responses"; r = −0.58 with GCS). Full text not opened; parameters confirmed from Peebles & Bothell (2004, ACT-R workshop paper, opened PDF): 225 trials, 25 of each digit, ≈4.3 min; and PsyToolkit's SART page (opened): digit 250 ms, mask 900 ms, five font sizes 48/72/94/100/120 pt, mask = circle with a cross, 18 practice + 225 trials.

**A4. Manly, T., Davison, B., Heutink, J., Galloway, M., & Robertson, I. H. (2000).** Not enough time or not enough attention? Speed, error and self-maintained control in the Sustained Attention to Response Test (SART). *Clinical Neuropsychological Assessment, 3*, 167–177. Not opened directly; its pooled data are reproduced in Peebles & Bothell (2004, opened): 109 healthy adults on the standard 225-trial SART: mean Go RT 375 ms (SD 65); commission errors 6.36/25 (SD 4.36) = **25.4 %**; omissions 1.06 (SD 3.41); r = −0.49 between RT and errors; pre-error RT 333 ms vs pre-correct 384 ms (4-trial windows).

**A5. Seli, P., Jonker, T. R., Cheyne, J. A., & Smilek, D. (2013).** Enhancing SART validity by statistically controlling speed–accuracy trade-offs. *Frontiers in Psychology, 4*, 265. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00265. PMC3651996 (opened, full text). 630 experimental + 18 practice trials, 250 ms digit / 900 ms mask, No-Go = 3, sizes 48–120 pt. Study 1 standard instructions (n = 60): **mean RT 351 ms, error rate 45 %**; "go slow" instructions: 438 ms, 32 %. Visual SART reanalysis: 367 ms / 40 %; auditory 432 ms / 34 %. Errors are "a systematic function of response speed".

**A6. Seli, P., Cheyne, J. A., & Smilek, D. (2012).** Attention failures versus misplaced diligence: Separating attention lapses from speed–accuracy trade-offs. *Consciousness and Cognition, 21*(1), 277–291. doi:10.1016/j.concog.2011.09.017. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Accuracy-only instructions reduce errors; U-shaped RT–error relation.

**A7. Cheyne, J. A., Solman, G. J. F., Carriere, J. S. A., & Smilek, D. (2009).** Anatomy of an error: A bidirectional state model of task engagement/disengagement and attention-related errors. *Cognition, 111*(1), 98–113. doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2008.12.009. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Three-state model; RT variability, anticipations, omissions as indicators; lag-sequential analyses around No-Go errors.

**A8. Smilek, D., Carriere, J. S. A., & Cheyne, J. A. (2010).** Failures of sustained attention in life, lab, and brain: Ecological validity of the SART. *Neuropsychologia, 48*(9), 2564–2570. doi:10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2010.05.002. Author PDF (opened). Meta-analytic CFQ–SART-error r = .21–.26; their version used 315 trials and a bull's-eye mask.

**A9. Helton, W. S. (2009).** Impulsive responding and the sustained attention to response task. *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology, 31*(1), 39–47. doi:10.1080/13803390801978856. Europe PMC abstract (opened). SART is "sensitive to impulsive responding".

**A10. Dang, J. S., Figueroa, I. J., & Helton, W. S. (2018).** You are measuring the decision to be fast, not inattention: The Sustained Attention to Response Task does not measure sustained attention. *Experimental Brain Research, 236*(8), 2255–2262. doi:10.1007/s00221-018-5291-6. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Within-subject speed–accuracy trade-off explains SART performance.

**A11. Dinges, D. F., & Powell, J. W. (1985).** Microcomputer analyses of performance on a portable, simple visual RT task during sustained operations. *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers, 17*(6), 652–655. doi:10.3758/BF03200977. Author PDF (opened, fatiguemanagersnetwork.org). Describes the Wilkinson VRT device: 4-digit millisecond counter, 10-min trials, **ISI random 1–10 s**, 1.5 s to read the RT, RTs ≤ 100 ms treated as false starts, analysis of slowest and fastest 10 %. **It does not define a 500-ms lapse and does not use a 2–10 s ISI.**

**A12. Basner, M., & Dinges, D. F. (2011).** Maximizing sensitivity of the psychomotor vigilance test (PVT) to sleep loss. *Sleep, 34*(5), 581–591. PMC3079937 (opened, full text). 10-min PVT, ISI 2–10 s, lapse = RT ≥ 500 ms, false start = response without stimulus or RT < 100 ms. Effect sizes (TSD): lapses + false starts 1.94, mean 1/RT 1.93, lapses 1.86, slowest 10 % 1/RT 1.86; **median RT 0.38 and mean RT 0.64 were the least sensitive**. Recommends mean 1/RT and number of lapses as primary outcomes.

**A13. Basner, M., Mollicone, D., & Dinges, D. F. (2011).** Validity and sensitivity of a brief psychomotor vigilance test (PVT-B) to total and partial sleep deprivation. *Acta Astronautica, 69*(11–12), 949–959. doi:10.1016/j.actaastro.2011.07.015. Author PDF (opened, med.upenn.edu). PVT-B: 3 min, **ISI 1–4 s "including a 1 s RT feedback interval"**; 74 subjects aged 22–45; 355-ms threshold "chosen because, under comparable conditions … it raised PVT-B lapse frequency to levels observed in the 10-min PVT with the standard 500 ms lapse definition"; PVT-B samples 62.3 vs 93.6 stimuli; effect size largest for mean 1/RT; average 22.7 % drop in effect size vs 10-min PVT.

**A14. Antler, C. A., Yamazaki, E. M., Casale, C. E., Brieva, T. E., & Goel, N. (2022).** The 3-minute psychomotor vigilance test demonstrates inadequate convergent validity relative to the 10-minute psychomotor vigilance test across sleep loss and recovery. *Frontiers in Neuroscience, 16*, 815697. doi:10.3389/fnins.2022.815697. PMC8885985 (opened). N = 83, age 34.7 ± 8.9. Baseline (rested) medians (IQR): PVT-10 lapses 0.00 (1.00), 1/RT 4.03 (0.75) and 3.88 (0.74) s⁻¹ (≈ 248–258 ms); PVT-3 (355 ms) lapses 1.00 (3.00), 1/RT 4.31 (0.78) and 4.12 (0.97) s⁻¹ (≈ 232–243 ms). PVT-10 ISI 2–10 s; PVT-3 ISI 1–4 s.

**A15. Grant, D. A., Honn, K. A., Layton, M. E., Riedy, S. M., & Van Dongen, H. P. A. (2017).** 3-minute smartphone-based and tablet-based psychomotor vigilance tests … *Behavior Research Methods, 49*(3), 1020–1029. doi:10.3758/s13428-016-0763-8. Europe PMC abstract (opened). RTs faster on touch devices than laptop; "loss of sensitivity on the 3-min PVTs may be mitigated by modifying the threshold defining lapses" (device-specific thresholds).

**A16. Seli, P., Cheyne, J. A., & Smilek, D. (2013).** Wandering minds and wavering rhythms: Linking mind wandering and behavioral variability. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 39*(1), 1–5. doi:10.1037/a0030954. Europe PMC abstract (opened). MRT introduced; "response variability during the 5 trials preceding probe-caught reports of mind wandering (tuned-out and zoned-out) is significantly greater than during the 5 trials preceding reports of on-task performance"; replicated across two samples. Probe categories: on task / tuned out / zoned out.

**A17. Seli, P., Carriere, J. S. A., Levene, M., & Smilek, D. (2013).** How few and far between? Examining the effects of probe rate on self-reported mind wandering. *Frontiers in Psychology, 4*, 430. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00430 (opened, full text). MRT, tone every 1,300 ms, 18 practice + 600 trials, 5–25 probes per participant (≈ 30 s to ≈ 3 min apart), N = 101. MW reported "about 46 % of the time" at ≈ 30-s spacing and "about 79 %" at ≈ 3-min spacing; r = .26. RRT variability = ln(variance) over a moving 5-trial window: on-task 8.09 (SD 1.10), MW 8.34 (SD 0.91), overall 8.24 (SD 0.66).

**A18. Anderson, T., Petranker, R., Lin, H., & Farb, N. A. S. (2021).** The metronome response task for measuring mind wandering: Replication attempt and extension of three studies by Seli et al. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 83*(1), 315–330. doi:10.3758/s13414-020-02131-x. Europe PMC abstract (opened). N = 300 multisite; RRT variability "reliably predicted self-reported mind-wandering".

**A19. Safati, A. B., Carr, T. H., Lowe, C. J., & Smilek, D. (2024).** Mind wandering on command. *Frontiers in Psychology, 15*, 1448226. PMC11412258 (opened). Documents the standard MRT trial: "650 ms of silence followed by the 75 ms tone, and another 575 ms of silence" = 1,300 ms.

**A20. Anderson, T., & Farb, N. A. S. (2020).** The Metronome Counting Task for measuring meta-awareness. *Behavior Research Methods, 52*(6), 2646–2656. doi:10.3758/s13428-020-01418-z. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Tap to a beat while counting to 20; special key marks the final count; self-caught failures; N = 74.

**A21. Levinson, D. B., Stoll, E. L., Kindy, S. D., Merry, H. L., & Davidson, R. J. (2014).** A mind you can count on: Validating breath counting as a behavioral measure of mindfulness. *Frontiers in Psychology, 5*, 1202. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01202 (opened, full text). Count breaths 1–9, key A on 1–8, key B on 9, key C = self-caught miscount/reset; sessions 15–30 min; test–retest ICC .60; meditators ≈ 84 % vs novices ≈ 76 % accuracy.

**A22. Chu, M. T., Marks, E., Smith, C. L., & Chadwick, P. (2023).** Self-caught methodologies for measuring mind wandering with meta-awareness: A systematic review. *Consciousness and Cognition, 108*, 103463. doi:10.1016/j.concog.2022.103463. Europe PMC abstract (opened). 39 studies; three task families incl. "breath or beat-focusing exercises".

**A23. Smallwood, J., & Schooler, J. W. (2006).** The restless mind. *Psychological Bulletin, 132*(6), 946–958. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.946. Europe PMC abstract (opened).

**A24. Smallwood, J., McSpadden, M., & Schooler, J. W. (2007).** The lights are on but no one's home: Meta-awareness and the decoupling of attention when the mind wanders. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 14*(3), 527–533. doi:10.3758/BF03194102. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Origin of the tune-out (aware) vs zone-out (unaware) distinction; unaware MW → short RTs and inhibition failures.

**A25. Schooler, J. W., Smallwood, J., Christoff, K., Handy, T. C., Reichle, E. D., & Sayette, M. A. (2011).** Meta-awareness, perceptual decoupling and the wandering mind. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 15*(7), 319–326. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2011.05.006. Europe PMC abstract (opened).

**A26. Smallwood, J., & Schooler, J. W. (2015).** The science of mind wandering: Empirically navigating the stream of consciousness. *Annual Review of Psychology, 66*, 487–518. doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010814-015331. Europe PMC abstract (opened).

**A27. Jackson, J. D., & Balota, D. A. (2012).** Mind-wandering in younger and older adults: Converging evidence from the Sustained Attention to Response Task and reading for comprehension. *Psychology and Aging, 27*(1), 106–119. doi:10.1037/a0023933. PMC3508668 (opened, full text). Exp. 2–3 probe: "Please choose the one option … (1) I was thinking about the task; (2) My mind was blank; (3) My mind drifted to things other than the task, but I wasn't aware of it until you asked me [zone out]; (4) While doing the task I was aware that thoughts about other things popped into my head [tune out]". Their SART used 200-ms digits, 900-ms ring-with-cross mask, 216/275 trials. Younger adults' MW: 21 % (Exp 1), 41 % (Exp 2), 72 % (Exp 3).

**A28. Weinstein, Y. (2018).** Mind-wandering, how do I measure thee with probes? Let me count the ways. *Behavior Research Methods, 50*(2), 642–661. doi:10.3758/s13428-017-0891-9. Europe PMC abstract (opened). "145 studies from 105 articles published between 2005 and 2015"; five methodologies; "at least 69 different methodological variants".

**A29. Robison, M. K., Miller, A. L., & Unsworth, N. (2019).** Examining the effects of probe frequency, response options, and framing within the thought-probe method. *Behavior Research Methods, 51*(1), 398–408. doi:10.3758/s13428-019-01212-6. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Probe frequency and framing did not change behaviour or probe responses in their experiments; recommends ≥ 3 options (on-task / off-task / task-related interference).

**A30. Lindquist, S. I., & McLean, J. P. (2011).** Daydreaming and its correlates in an educational environment. *Learning and Individual Differences, 21*(2), 158–167. ERIC EJ917064 abstract (opened). 463 students, three lectures, five probes per lecture; no overall MW percentage in the abstract.

**A31. Risko, E. F., Anderson, N., Sarwal, A., Engelhardt, M., & Kingstone, A. (2012).** Everyday attention: Variation in mind wandering and memory in a lecture. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 26*(2), 234–242. doi:10.1002/acp.1814. Crossref abstract (opened): "mind wandering increases with time on task and memory for the lecture material decreases". No percentages available to me.

**A32. Farley, J., Risko, E. F., & Kingstone, A. (2013).** Everyday attention and lecture retention: The effects of time, fidgeting, and mind wandering. *Frontiers in Psychology, 4*, 619 (opened, full text). 40-min lecture in eight 5-min blocks; attention rating (7-point) declined with block (β = −0.18); no dichotomous MW rates.

**A33. Wammes, J. D., Boucher, P. O., Seli, P., Cheyne, J. A., & Smilek, D. (2016).** Mind wandering during lectures I: Changes in rates across an entire semester. *Scholarship of Teaching and Learning in Psychology, 2*(1), 13–32. doi:10.1037/stl0000053. Primary not opened (APA page did not render). Summarised in Pachai, Acai, LoGiudice & Kim (2016, *Scholarship of Teaching and Learning in Psychology 2*(2), 134–146; author PDF opened): N = 154, > 5,000 observations; "unintentional mind wandering rates occurred only 14 % of the time, and … mind wandering did not increase over time during the lectures".

**A34. Varao-Sousa, T. L., & Kingstone, A. (2019).** Are mind wandering rates an artifact of the probe-caught method? *Behavior Research Methods, 51*(1), 235–242. doi:10.3758/s13428-018-1073-0. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Self-caught MW in real lectures is stable and unaffected by adding probes.

**A35. Block, R. A., Hancock, P. A., & Zakay, D. (2010).** How cognitive load affects duration judgments: A meta-analytic review. *Acta Psychologica, 134*(3), 330–343. doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2010.03.006. Europe PMC abstract (opened). 117 experiments: "With higher cognitive load, the prospective duration judgment ratio (subjective/objective) decreases but the retrospective ratio increases"; prospective CV rises with load.

**A36. Block, R. A., & Zakay, D. (1997).** Prospective and retrospective duration judgments: A meta-analytic review. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 4*(2), 184–197. doi:10.3758/BF03209393. Europe PMC abstract (opened).

**A37. Zakay, D., & Block, R. A. (1995).** An attentional-gate model of prospective time estimation. In M. Richelle, V. De Keyser, G. d'Ydewalle & A. Vandierendonck (Eds.), *Time and the dynamic control of behavior* (pp. 167–178). Liège: Université de Liège. **Not opened** (montana.edu PDF returned 403; Semantic Scholar record exists). Direction of effect confirmed instead from A35.

**A38. Wilson, K., & Korn, J. H. (2007).** Attention during lectures: Beyond ten minutes. *Teaching of Psychology, 34*(2), 85–89. doi:10.1080/00986280701291291. Crossref abstract (opened). Insufficient evidence for the 10–15-min claim.

**A39. Bradbury, N. A. (2016).** Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more? *Advances in Physiology Education, 40*(4), 509–513. doi:10.1152/advan.00109.2016. Europe PMC abstract (opened). "the available primary data do not support the concept of a 10- to 15-min attention limit"; largest variance is between teachers.

**A40. Bunce, D. M., Flens, E. A., & Neiles, K. Y. (2010).** How long can students pay attention in class? A study of student attention decline using clickers. *Journal of Chemical Education, 87*(12), 1438–1443. doi:10.1021/ed100409p. ERIC abstract (opened): lapses of "1 min or less more often than longer"; cycles shorten as lecture proceeds; clicker questions and demonstrations lower self-reported decline, persisting into following segments. Faculty Focus summary (opened) quoting p. 1442: "lapses of 30 seconds after the class began, followed by more lapses about four-and-a-half minutes into the lecture".

**A41. Nuechterlein, K. H., Parasuraman, R., & Jiang, Q. (1983).** Visual sustained attention: Image degradation produces rapid sensitivity decrement over time. *Science, 220*(4594), 327–329. doi:10.1126/science.6836276. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Large sensitivity declines "after only 5 minutes" — but only with highly degraded stimuli.

**A42. Temple, J. G., Warm, J. S., Dember, W. N., Jones, K. S., LaGrange, C. M., & Matthews, G. (2000).** The effects of signal salience and caffeine on performance, workload, and stress in an abbreviated vigilance task. *Human Factors, 42*(2), 183–194. doi:10.1518/001872000779656480. Europe PMC abstract (opened). A 12-min high-workload task reproduces the decrement.

**A43. Anwyl-Irvine, A., Dalmaijer, E. S., Hodges, N., & Evershed, J. K. (2021).** Realistic precision and accuracy of online experiment platforms, web browsers, and devices. *Behavior Research Methods, 53*(4), 1407–1425. doi:10.3758/s13428-020-01501-5. PMC8367876 (opened, full text). RT delay by platform: Gorilla 78.5 ms (SD 8.3), Lab.js 71.3 (SD 28.2), PsychoJS 82.3 (SD 16.4), jsPsych 87.4 (SD 15.3); by browser 76.5–82.3 ms (SD 18.5–21.9); "around 80 ms" overall, "to 100 ms on some set-ups". Visual duration delay 9.8–30 ms depending on browser/OS. Recommends within-participant designs.

**A44. Bridges, D., Pitiot, A., MacAskill, M. R., & Peirce, J. W. (2020).** The timing mega-study: Comparing a range of experiment generators, both lab-based and online. *PeerJ, 8*, e9414. doi:10.7717/peerj.9414. Europe PMC abstract (opened). Online RT precision "at least under 10 ms in all browsers" for most packages, PsychoPy < 3.5 ms; lags larger than lab but consistent.

**A45. Gloria Mark's screen-switching figures.** University of California news, "Can't pay attention? You're not alone" (opened): 2004 ≈ 2.5 min per screen; 2012 = 75 s; last five or six years = 47 s; measured by computer logging; others replicated (50 s, 44 s; median 40 s). Book: Mark, G. (2023). *Attention Span*. Hanover Square Press (not opened).

**A46. Goldfish claim.** McSpadden, K. (14 May 2015). "You now have a shorter attention span than a goldfish". *Time* (opened): Microsoft Canada consumer-insights report, 2,000 surveyed + 112 EEG; 12 s (2000) → 8 s; goldfish 9 s. The BBC investigation (Maybin, S., 10 March 2017, "Busting the attention span myth", bbc.com/news/health-38896790) **could not be fetched from this environment**; that Statistic Brain was the cited source and could produce none is reported consistently by the secondary pieces I opened (Fast Company, Sword and the Script) and by A39's title framing.

## C. Design audit by instrument

### C1. Instrument I — The Second Hand (count & checkpoints; skip watch)

**Count-and-checkpoint mode.** Not a published paradigm. Its components map onto: (a) self-caught mind-wandering reports (A23, A25, A34); (b) counting tasks with a self-caught reset key — the Breath Counting Task (A21: keys for counts 1–8, 9, and "lost count") and the Metronome Counting Task (A20: tap the beat, count to 20, self-catch); (c) prospective duration judgment under load (A35). The checkpoint rule (tracking ≤ 5 % or ≤ 3 s; slipping ≤ 15 %) has no published analogue; BCT scores a set as correct or not and reports accuracy ≈ 76–84 % over 15–30 min.

What to change or say:
- Cite A21 and A20 for the count-and-self-catch design, A23/A24/A25 for "self-caught vs probe-caught" and tune-out/zone-out, A22 for the family of self-caught tasks.
- State plainly in the Guide that the stretch bands (45 s / 2 min / 5 min) are the author's heuristics, not research norms; there is no published distribution of self-caught intervals for a second-hand count. If a norm is wanted, the closest is BCT accuracy (~76 % novices vs ~84 % meditators, A21), which is a different unit.
- The "counting rate" reading should not be framed as a diagnostic of divided attention (B23). Chronometric counting is exactly the strategy timing researchers suppress, so the attentional-gate literature applies only loosely.
- Score: the "stretch" half of the score is uncalibrated by construction; consider weighting checkpoints more than stretches, or reporting stretches without a 0–100 mapping.

**Skip-watch (Mackworth) mode.** Signal = a two-tick jump (matches Mackworth's "double jump", A1). Signal density is far higher than Mackworth's: first at 8–20 s, then every 12–45 s ⇒ ≈ 8–12 signals in 5 min versus 12 per **30 min** in 1948 (≈ 0.4/min vs 10–20× denser). Response window 2.5 s (PsyToolkit uses 1 s; Mackworth's window not verified). Session 3–15 min versus 2 h. So this is a *demonstration* of the task, not a vigilance test in Mackworth's rare-signal regime; the first-half/second-half comparison is a reasonable exploratory measure but the reading's "vigilance decrement … shows up here within minutes" should be softened (B2). If the teacher wants a decrement, the literature's shortest validated forms are 12 min with high workload (A42) or 5 min with degraded signals (A41): reduce signal salience (smaller jump, 1-s window) rather than increase signal count.

### C2. Instrument II — Not the Three (SART)

Design: 250-ms digit, 900-ms mask (ring + X), SOA 1,150 ms, 18 practice + 225 trials in shuffled blocks of nine ⇒ exactly 25 No-Go; five sizes 48–120 (px, scaled). This matches the Robertson protocol as described in every secondary I opened (B5). The 90-trial option gives only 10 No-Go trials; label its commission rate as coarse (10 % steps). The pre-error analysis (mean RT over the 4 Go trials before each No-Go) matches Robertson/Manly's window (A4).

Norms and bands:
- Standard 225-trial SART, healthy adults (n = 109): commission 25.4 % (SD 17.4 %), Go RT 375 ms (SD 65), omissions ≈ 1/200 (A4).
- Undergraduates, 630-trial SART, standard instructions: 45 %, 351 ms; accuracy-emphasis instructions: 32 %, 438 ms (A5).
- Jackson & Balota younger adults (216–275 trials, 200-ms digits): MW rates 21–41 % of probes; error rates not extracted.

Concretely: (1) replace "about 45 %" with the two-anchor statement in B6; (2) re-cut bands so the healthy mean is not the edge of "sharp" — e.g. ≤ 12 % sharp, 12–40 % typical, > 40 % speed-dominated; (3) add an RT-adjusted line ("your errors given your speed", A5) or at least print the speed–error trade-off explicitly: "faster than ~350 ms Go RT with high errors usually means a speed strategy, not lapses"; (4) instruct "accuracy first" if the intent is to measure lapses (A6); (5) soften the "autopilot signature" language (B7, B8) and cite Dang et al. 2018 / Helton 2009 in the Guide caveats.

### C3. Instrument III — The Counter (PVT / PVT-B)

Design vs source:
- 3-min PVT-B: game ISI 1–4 s **plus** a 900-ms feedback display (effective 1.9–4.9 s from the previous response); Basner's 1–4 s *includes* the 1-s feedback. Fix: `arm(feedbackUntil - 900 + rand(1000,4000))` or simply draw ISI from 100–3,100 ms after feedback so the response-to-stimulus interval is 1–4 s. Stimulus count should then approach Basner's ≈ 62 per 3 min (currently ≈ 45–50).
- 10-min: ISI 2–10 s + 900 ms feedback (2.9–10.9 s); same fix. Lapse ≥ 500 ms, false start (no stimulus or RT < 100 ms) match A12. Timeout 10 s vs Basner's 30 s — fine, but count it as a lapse (it does).
- 5-min option uses the 10-min rules; no source validates a 5-min/500-ms variant (A15 used 3-min with device-specific thresholds). Say so, or drop it.

Outcomes: report **mean 1/RT** (already computed as `speed`) and lapses as the primary pair (A12); median RT is the least sleep-sensitive metric and the score should not weight it 40 %. Suggested score: 0.6 × lapse-rate band + 0.4 × response-speed band (e.g. 4.3 s⁻¹ → 100, 3.5 → 60, 2.5 → 20), or drop the RT term and use lapses + false starts (highest effect size in A12).

Norms: rested adults on lab hardware — lapses median 0 (IQR 1) in 10 min, median 1 (IQR 3) in 3 min at 355 ms; 1/RT ≈ 3.9–4.3 s⁻¹ (A14). Browser/keyboard adds ≈ 70–100 ms (A43) with SD 8–30 ms; on a touch screen RTs run faster than on a laptop (A15). Consequences: (a) the 355-ms threshold, chosen to equate lapse *frequency* on lab hardware, will over-count lapses in a browser by design; (b) the median-RT band (230 → 100) is unreachable for most students (B29). Options: keep thresholds but say in the Guide that a browser baseline of ≈ 300–350 ms median and 3–8 "lapses" is normal for a rested student; or let the student set a personal baseline run and score subsequent runs relative to it (A43's within-participant advice).

### C4. Instrument IV — The Metronome (MRT)

Design vs source: 1,300-ms trial, 75-ms tone (A17, A19) — matches. Seli's tone sits at 650 ms into the trial; the game's tone is at the beat and taps are scored ±650 ms around it — equivalent. Probe spacing 23–46 beats (30–60 s) is at the dense end of A17's range (30 s–3 min) — fine, but the Guide should note that denser probes lower reported wandering (A17). 3-min default ≈ 138 beats vs 600 trials (13 min) in the source studies; acceptable for a demo. Local 5-tap SD before each probe matches A16's 5-trial window (they used ln-variance; SD is fine for students).

Probe options: A16 used on task / tuned out / zoned out; Jackson & Balota used task / blank / zone-out / tune-out. Keep the aware/unaware split; replace "Not sure" with "Mind blank" or "Thinking about how I'm doing" (task-related interference, A29) if you want a published fourth option.

Bands: the only published variability data are log-variances (A17); back-transformed, typical 5-tap SD is ≈ 57 ms on-task and ≈ 65 ms before wandering in undergraduates on lab keyboards, and A18 reports MW increases variability by roughly a third (A19: 31 % higher under 80 % vs 20 % MW instructions). Whole-run SD (the headline) includes drift and browser jitter (8–30 ms, A43), so it will be higher still. The bands < 45 / 45–80 / > 80 ms would classify the typical student as "wavering". Suggest: report the local-SD contrast (wandering vs on-task probes) as the headline finding, and either widen bands (e.g. < 60 steady, 60–100 wavering, > 100 erratic, flagged as provisional) or score jitter relative to the student's first-minute baseline. Also state that mean offset (early/late bias) is not an attention measure (the reading already says this — good).

### C5. Thought probes (all instruments)

Wording "Just before this appeared, where was your attention?" is a neutral prompt (A28 "neutral" class). Four options: see C4. Reported rates depend on spacing and wording (A17, A28) but not always (A29) — say both. Distinguish "aware" (tune-out) and "unaware" (zone-out) as in A24; the results text already does this well.

## D. Statements no paper supports — remove or soften

1. "Lecture studies catch mind-wandering on 30–40 % of probes, rising toward half late in an hour" (README; Guide). Not found in any opened source; the largest classroom study found 14 % unintentional and no rise (A33 via Pachai). Reword per B22.
2. "Browsers add 8–67 ms of latency with under 1–8 ms precision" (README). The cited paper reports ≈ 70–100 ms delay and 8–28 ms SD (A43). Correct the numbers.
3. "Dinges & Powell 1985 … 2–10 s waits, lapses ≥ 500 ms". The 1985 paper has 1–10 s and no 500-ms rule (A11). Re-attribute to Basner & Dinges 2011.
4. "In studies of university students the average is about 45 % under these instructions" (SART reading) — true only for 630-trial Waterloo samples; on this game's 225-trial format the pooled healthy-adult mean is ≈ 25 % (A4). Reword per B6.
5. "Pressing on a 3 is the signature of a mind running on autopilot" / "Speeding up is the fingerprint of autopilot" — contested; present as one interpretation (A5, A6, A9, A10).
6. "The vigilance decrement … typically sets in within the first half hour and shows up here within minutes" — the second clause is an extrapolation (A41, A42 cover 5–12 min only for demanding tasks).
7. "Attention wanders long before behaviour collapses, and it shows first as jitter" (MRT lede) — not what A16/A18 show; they show an association in the 6.5 s before a probe.
8. "a slow count is itself a soft sign of divided attention" — the direction is right (A35) but the inference from an explicit count is loose; say "consistent with".
9. "Reaction steadiness (CV) under 0.20 is steady"; MRT "< 45 ms steady"; clock stretch bands; "a clean run of 5 minutes on a second hand is genuinely rare"; PVT median-RT score anchors — all house heuristics; the Guide's sentence "built from the same research bands" and README's "piecewise-linear map of the same research bands" should say "author-chosen bands informed by the literature", because only the PVT lapse counts and the SART error range have published anchors.
10. "Seli, Cheyne & Smilek 2013 … 46 % vs 79 %" — wrong paper (A17 is Seli, Carriere, Levene & Smilek).
11. "The four-option format … follows Smallwood & Schooler and Jackson & Balota (2012)" — the "not sure" option is not theirs (B21).
