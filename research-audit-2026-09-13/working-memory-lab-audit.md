# Working Memory Lab: research audit

Audit of `working-memory-lab.html` (Guide, ledes, readings, score bands) and the README section "## Working Memory Lab" against primary sources. Checked 13 September 2026. Every quoted number below was read from a source I opened; where I could not open a source I say so and mark the claim **UNVERIFIED**.

Verdict key: **MATCHES** (numbers and design as in the source), **OVERSTATED** (directionally right, stronger than the source), **WRONG** (contradicts the source), **UNSUPPORTED** (no source found; claim may be an interpretation), **UNVERIFIED** (primary not opened).

## A. Verified citations

Each entry: reference, what I opened, one line on what it establishes.

1. **Miller, G. A. (1956).** The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information. *Psychological Review, 63*(2), 81–97. doi:10.1037/h0043158. Opened: full text at https://www.musanim.com/miller1956/. Establishes: span of immediate memory is about seven items and "almost independent of the number of bits per chunk"; recoding into larger chunks raises the information held; Miller closes by calling the recurring seven possibly "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence". He does *not* say the seven is "a span with rehearsal allowed"; that framing is Cowan's.

2. **Cowan, N. (2001).** The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24*(1), 87–114 (discussion 114–185). doi:10.1017/S0140525X01003922. PMID 11515286. Opened: abstract via NCBI E-utilities. Establishes: Miller's seven "was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit"; the "more precise" limit is "only three to five chunks"; "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks". Four boundary conditions for seeing it: (1) information overload limits chunks to single items, (2) recoding into larger chunks is blocked, (3) performance discontinuities, (4) indirect effects. Under these, "rehearsal and long-term memory cannot be used to combine stimulus items into chunks".

3. **Woods, D. L., Kishiyama, M. M., Yund, E. W., Herron, T. J., Edwards, B., Poliva, O., Hink, R. F., & Reed, B. (2011).** Improving digit span assessment of short-term verbal memory. *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology, 33*(1), 101–111. doi:10.1080/13803395.2010.493149. PMC2978794. Opened: full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2978794/. Establishes: spoken digits over headphones at 1/s; Exp. 1 N = 29 (ages 18–46, mean 26, three sessions, adaptive 1:2 staircase, 14 trials): forward Mean Span (MS) 7.41 (SD 0.94), backward MS 6.00 (SD 1.32); two-error maximum length (TE-ML) 7.36 (0.88) / 5.80 (1.42) (Table 2). Exp. 2 N = 763 (ages 18–65, mean 46.5): forward MS 6.52 (1.00), backward MS 4.91 (1.06); TE-ML 6.35 (1.15) / 4.61 (1.22) (Table 6). Describes the conventional Wechsler procedure: lists start at two digits, two trials per length, testing stops when both trials at a length are failed or the maximum (9 forward, 8 backward) is reached. Quotes WMS-III norms for ages 20–29: forward 6.74, backward 5.07.

4. **Kahneman, D., & Beatty, J. (1966).** Pupil diameter and load on memory. *Science, 154*(3756), 1583–1585. doi:10.1126/science.154.3756.1583. PMID 5924930. Opened: abstract only (E-utilities); full text not opened. Establishes (abstract): "pupil diameter is a measure of the amount of material which is under active processing at any time. The pupil dilates as the material is presented and constricts during report. The rate of change of these functions is related to task difficulty."

5. **Kahneman, D. (2011).** *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, ch. 2 "Attention and Effort". Opened: full text PDF at https://mlsu.ac.in/econtents/2950_Daniel%20Kahneman%20-%20Thinking,%20Fast%20and%20Slow%20(2013).pdf. Establishes (verbatim): "set a metronome at 1/sec ... read the four digits aloud. Wait for two beats, then report a string in which each of the original digits is incremented by 1. If the digits on the card are 5294, the correct response is 6305. Keeping the rhythm is important. Few people can cope with more than four digits in the Add-1 task, but if you want a harder challenge, please try Add-3." On the 1966 work: "The beats triggered an infrared flash every second ... We recorded strings of digits on beats of the metronome and instructed the subject to repeat or transform the digits one by one, maintaining the same rhythm ... Add-1 with four digits caused a larger dilation than the task of holding seven digits for immediate recall. Add-3, which is much more difficult, is the most demanding that I ever observed. In the first 5 seconds, the pupil dilates by about 50% of its original area and heart rate increases by about 7 beats per minute." No explicit wrap rule is stated; 9 → 0 is only implied by the 5294 → 6305 example.

6. **Chase, W. G., & Simon, H. A. (1973).** Perception in chess. *Cognitive Psychology, 4*(1), 55–81. doi:10.1016/0010-0285(73)90004-2. Opened: full text PDF at http://matt.colorado.edu/teaching/highcog/fall8/cs73.pdf. Establishes: three subjects (master M, Class A player A, beginner B); positions viewed for 5 s, reconstructed on a blank board; middle-game positions with 24–26 pieces: M placed "about 16 pieces correctly on the first trial, while A and B placed about eight and four"; end-game (12–15 pieces): about 8, 7, 4. "In the random, unstructured positions there was no relation at all between memory of the position and playing strength", and all three did worse on random positions than B did on real ones. Reports de Groot's masters replacing 23–24 of 25 pieces.

7. **Simon, H. A. (1974).** How big is a chunk? *Science, 183*(4124), 482–488. doi:10.1126/science.183.4124.482. Opened: full text PDF at https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Simon1974.pdf. Establishes (Table 1, author as sole subject, lists read aloud at ~2 items/s): 1-syllable words: 7 words = 7 chunks; 2-syllable: 7 = 7; 3-syllable: 6 = 6; 2-word phrases: 9 words = 4 chunks (text: "Four or five seems to be about the limit"); 8-word phrases: 22 words = 3 chunks ("Lists of three such phrases were all I could recall with reliability, although I could sometimes retain four"). "the number of chunks retained declines only by a factor of two, while the number of words retained increases by a factor of three".

8. **Bower, G. H., & Springston, F. (1970).** Pauses as recoding points in letter series. *Journal of Experimental Psychology, 83*(3, Pt. 1), 421–430. doi:10.1037/h0028863. **Not opened** (PsycNet, ResearchGate and course pages all blocked; Semantic Scholar record has no abstract). Secondary teaching sources consistently give the example strings "FBI PHD TWA IBM" vs "FB IPH DTW AIB M"; one student page lists "FBI, PHD, CIA, IBM" while still giving the control letters as "FB, IPH, DTW, AIB, M" (which are the letters of FBI PHD TWA IBM, not of a CIA list).

9. **Ericsson, K. A., Chase, W. G., & Faloon, S. (1980).** Acquisition of a memory skill. *Science, 208*(4448), 1181–1182. doi:10.1126/science.7375930. PMID 7375930. Opened: abstract (E-utilities): "After more than 230 hours of practice in the laboratory, a subject was able to increase his memory span from 7 to 79 digits." Consonant span: from **Chase, W. G., & Ericsson, K. A. (1981).** *Skilled memory* (Carnegie-Mellon technical report, DTIC ADA114635; opened via https://mlc-wels.edu/continuing-education/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2024/06/ADA114635-PDS0105-5.pdf): "after 3 months of practice on the digit span task, we tested SF's memory span for consonants, and he showed no transfer at all from the digit-span task and his consonant span was around 6"; elsewhere "for random consonants, SF's memory span is normal, about 7 symbols."

10. **Luck, S. J., & Vogel, E. K. (1997).** The capacity of visual working memory for features and conjunctions. *Nature, 390*(6657), 279–281. doi:10.1038/36846. Opened: full text PDF at https://awhvogellab.com/files/pdfs/luck_1997_capacity-features-conjuctions.pdf. Establishes: sample array of 1–12 coloured squares for 100 ms, 900-ms blank, test array 2,000 ms "either identical to the sample array or differed in the colour of one of the squares" (whole-display test; change on 50% of trials); set sizes 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 12; 7 colours (red, blue, violet, green, yellow, black, white); 10 college students per experiment, 32–40 trials per condition; "observers were able to retain the colours of roughly four items" (Pashler's estimate); 100 vs 500 ms sample duration "not significantly influenced"; a cue-box (single-decision) variant at set sizes 4, 8, 12 gave the same result; verbal load did not change performance.

11. **Rouder, J. N., Morey, R. D., Morey, C. C., & Cowan, N. (2011).** How to measure working memory capacity in the change detection paradigm. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 18*(2), 324–330. doi:10.3758/s13423-011-0055-3. PMC3070885. Opened: full text PDF at https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/15165212/Rouder_etal_2011_pbr.pdf. Establishes: Pashler's k_p = N(h − f)/(1 − f) for whole-display; Cowan's k_c = N(h − f) for single-probe; "Measure k_p is principled for whole-display recognition. Measure k_c is principled for single-probe recognition. Conversely, k_p is unprincipled for single-probe recognition; k_c is unprincipled for whole-display recognition."

12. **Luck, S. J., & Vogel, E. K. (2013).** Visual working memory capacity: from psychophysics and neurobiology to individual differences. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 17*(8), 391–400. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2013.06.006. PMC3729738. Opened: PMC page. Establishes: "When memory for simple, highly discriminable colored squares is tested, the typical college student has a capacity of only 3–4 objects' worth of information."

13. **Xu, Z., Adam, K. C. S., Fang, X., & Vogel, E. K. (2018).** The reliability and stability of visual working memory capacity. *Behavior Research Methods, 50*(2), 576–588. doi:10.3758/s13428-017-0886-6. PMC5632133. Opened: full text. Establishes: Exp. 1 N = 137 university students (mean age 19.97), single-probe change detection, array 150 ms, retention 1,000 ms, set sizes 4/6/8, nine colours, 540 trials: K = 2.32 (SD .70) at 4, 2.10 (.97) at 6, 1.98 (.97) at 8, mean 2.14 (Table 1); K = N × (H − FA), "Cowan's formula is best for single-probe displays like the one employed here". Exp. 2 (N = 79, 31 sessions over 60 days): K rose from 2.15 on day 1 to 3.02 on day 30 (practice effect).

14. **Peterson, L. R., & Peterson, M. J. (1959).** Short-term retention of individual verbal items. *Journal of Experimental Psychology, 58*(3), 193–198. doi:10.1037/h0049234. Opened: full text PDF at https://labs.la.utexas.edu/gilden/files/2016/03/1545ca4cdad4f480f2fab8fd505a5b7f7603.pdf (pages rendered and read). Establishes: 24 students; 48 consonant syllables spelled aloud by E, then a three-digit number; "The S then counted backward by three or four from this number" (by three on half the trials, by four on the other half); "E spoke in rhythm with a metronome clicking twice per second and S was instructed to do likewise"; intervals 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 s, eight trials each; materials chosen to be "categorically dissimilar and hence involve a minimum of interference". Fig. 2 (all latencies, asymptotes read from the figure): ~.80 at 3 s, ~.57 at 6 s, ~.32 at 9 s, ~.22 at 12 s, ~.11 at 15 s, ~.09 at 18 s. Fig. 3 (only responses with latency < 2.83 s, the curve usually reproduced): ~.53, ~.40, ~.23, ~.12, ~.09, ~.06, fitted P(t) = .89[.01 + .99(.85)^t]. Exp. 2 (Table 1): allowing 3 s of spoken rehearsal before counting raised recall at 3/9/18 s from .60/.25/.14 to .80/.48/.34. No condition in which S simply waits for the whole interval.

15. **Keppel, G., & Underwood, B. J. (1962).** Proactive inhibition in short-term retention of single items. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1*(3), 153–161. doi:10.1016/S0022-5371(62)80023-1. **Not opened** (ScienceDirect blocked; no abstract on Semantic Scholar). Described in two open papers I did open: Neath & Brown (2012, PMC3278777): "Keppel and Underwood (1962) ... observed no difference in performance in the various delay conditions on the first trial of a Brown–Peterson task"; Lin & Luck (2012, PMC3289109): "recall was very good at long delays on the first trial (presumably because there was no interference from previous trials)."

16. **Hebb, D. O. (1961).** Distinctive features of learning in the higher animal. In J. F. Delafresnaye (Ed.), *Brain mechanisms and learning* (pp. 37–46). Oxford: Blackwell. **Not opened.** Described in Page & Norris (2009, below): "a nine-digit list repeatedly presented every third trial among a number of such lists ... became progressively better recalled even though the repetition was unannounced to participants and even among participants who claimed not to recognize that any repetition had occurred"; Szmalec et al. (2009): "one particular sequence of digits was repeated every third trial"; Paice et al. (2025, PMC11783982): "lists of nine digits". The figure "24 lists" appeared only in a search snippet of Musfeld & Oberauer (2026, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, doi:10.1177/17456916251408052; page blocked) and is **UNVERIFIED** from anything I opened.

17. **Page, M. P. A., & Norris, D. (2009).** A model linking immediate serial recall, the Hebb repetition effect and the learning of phonological word forms. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 364*(1536), 3737–3753. doi:10.1098/rstb.2009.0173. PMC2846317. Opened: PMC page. Establishes: "Most experiments investigating the Hebb repetition effect involve around eight repetitions of the repeating list, evenly distributed among 20 or so filler lists"; "performance, measured as the percentage of items recalled in the correct list position, will rise from a baseline of around 50 per cent at a rate of approximately 3–4% per repetition".

18. **Szmalec, A., Duyck, W., Vandierendonck, A., Mata, A. B., & Page, M. P. A. (2009).** The Hebb repetition effect as a laboratory analogue of novel word learning. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 62*(3), 435–443. doi:10.1080/17470210802386375. Opened: author PDF at https://users.ugent.be/~wduyck/articles/SzmalecDuyckVandierendonckBarberaMataPageInPressInPress.pdf. Establishes: sequences of **nine** CV syllables (fixed length, not span+1), two blocks of 36 trials, "the same sequence was repeated every third trial", 12 repetitions per block, 1,000 ms per item; Hebb gradient .026 per repetition vs .002 for fillers (≈ 2.6 points per repetition); cites Stadler (1993) that "explicit recognition of the repetition is irrelevant to, and unnecessary for, Hebb learning".

19. **Norris, D., Page, M. P. A., & Hall, J. (2018).** Learning nonwords: the Hebb repetition effect as a model of word learning. *Memory, 26*(6), 852–857. doi:10.1080/09658211.2017.1416639. PMC5954922. Opened: full text. Establishes: lists set "to one syllable greater than their span" (most participants span 7 → 8-syllable lists); **two** Hebb lists per half, first appearing on trials 3 and 5 of a six-trial block (each list recurs every sixth trial); "Each Hebb list was presented eight times in total"; 850 ms per syllable; effect measured as regression slopes; no discussion of awareness; no percentage gains reported.

20. **Araya, C., Oberauer, K., & Saito, S. (2022).** The Hebb repetition effect in complex span tasks. *Memory & Cognition, 50*(5), 925–940. doi:10.3758/s13421-021-01261-3. PMC9209383. Opened. Establishes: 8-consonant lists; Exp. 1 (8 repetitions, 24 trials) improved 12–19 percentage points; Exps 2–3 (7 reps) 6–15 points; Exp. 4 (6 reps) 4–11 points.

21. **Guérard, K., Saint-Aubin, J., Boucher, P., & Tremblay, S. (2011).** The role of awareness in anticipation and recall performance in the Hebb repetition paradigm. *Memory & Cognition, 39*(6), 1012–1022. doi:10.3758/s13421-011-0084-1. PMID 21448765. Opened: abstract. "Awareness does not affect learning a repeated sequence in the Hebb repetition paradigm, as is evidenced by recall performance ... recall performance for the repeated sequence improved across repetitions for all participants but ... anticipation increased only for participants aware of the repetition." Also **Couture & Tremblay (2006)**, *Memory & Cognition, 34*(8), 1720–1729, PMID 17489297 (abstract opened): "Repetition of a to-be-remembered sequence of verbal items every third trial markedly improves serial recall". 22. **Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. 23. **Rowland, C. A. (2014).** The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect. *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(6), 1432–1463. doi:10.1037/a0037559. ## C. Design audit by instrument

### I. Digit span (SpanTest)
Implemented: visual digits, 700 ms on / 300 ms off (1/s); ladder from 3 (or 4) to 12; one pass moves up, two fails at a length end the ladder; span = longest passed length; backward restarts short.
Against the literature: rate matches WAIS-IV and Woods (1/s). Differences: WAIS-IV starts at 2 digits, always gives both trials of each item, stops at 9 forward / 8 backward, and is spoken; Woods Exp. 1 used a 1:2 staircase (not the two-trial rule) and 14 trials. The game's "longest length with at least one pass" is the same quantity as the WAIS longest-digit-span score, so the span estimate is comparable; the total-correct score is not.
Change: (a) offer a start at 2 or 3 and note that 3 is a shortcut; (b) either give both trials at every length (to match Wechsler) or keep the ladder and say so; (c) fix the bands to forward 6–8 typical / backward 4–6 typical; (d) note that the norms quoted are for spoken digits.

### II. Add-1 / Add-3 (PlusTest)
Implemented: beat 1,000 ms (or 1,500); digits shown on the beat; "+k" cue for one beat then a second beat (two-beat pause); typed report one per beat with two grace beats; ladder 3 → 10; wrap mod 10.
Against Kahneman: 1/s metronome, four digits, two-beat wait, report on the rhythm: matches TFS. Deviations: Kahneman & Beatty used spoken digits and spoken report; the game is visual and typed (typing a digit within a 1-s beat is a motor demand the original did not have; the 1.5-s option is sensible). The ladder and the notion of an "add-one span" are the lab's invention; TFS only says few people cope with more than four digits. No norms exist. The lede's "one-beat pause" contradicts the Guide and the code.
Change: fix the lede; present the add-one/add-three "spans" as the lab's own measure; keep the comparison with the student's own forward span (a within-person contrast, not a literature quantity).

### III. Formula round (ChunkTest)
Implemented: one character at a time, 700 ms (546 ms on); scrambled lists 4 → 16 characters; formula lists 2 → 9 formulas (~4.5 characters per formula); same ladder; case-insensitive check with 0 → O and I → l.
Against the literature: Chase & Simon showed a whole board for 5 s and had subjects reconstruct it; recall was of simultaneous, spatial material; three subjects. The sequential letter-string design is closer to Bower & Springston (1970) (letter series whose grouping does or does not form known acronyms) and, for chemistry specifically, to Zhilin & Tkachuk (2013), who showed expert and novice chemists sequences of chemical symbols for 30 s and found experts better only on real equations. Simon (1974) read word lists at about 2 items/s. There is no published basis for 0.7 s per character; it is a reasonable choice.
Points to change or state: (a) cite Bower & Springston and Zhilin & Tkachuk as the design's nearest relatives and Chase & Simon as the idea's origin; (b) the "chunk factor" (formula characters ÷ scrambled characters) and the 70/30 score are the lab's own; (c) the scrambled ladder allows two tries per length like Instrument I, which is fine, but the formula ladder grows by whole formulas (about 4–5 characters per step), so a single miss costs more; consider growing by one formula but scoring characters held on the best passed list (already done) and saying so; (d) the "no two digits adjacent" rule and the bigram filter are sensible controls with no literature analogue.

### IV. Change detection (ChangeTest)
Implemented: fixation 600 ms; array 100 ms (or 500); blank 900 ms; single probe in a ring at its original location; 2/4/6/8 squares × 16 trials (8 change, 8 same) = 64; eight colours; K = N(H − F) per set size; reported K = mean over set sizes ≥ 4.
Against Luck & Vogel 1997 and Rouder 2011: 100 ms / 900 ms match; single probe with Cowan's K is the correct pairing; 50% change rate matches. Deviations: L&V used set sizes 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 12 with seven colours and a whole-display test; Xu et al. used 4/6/8 with 150 ms / 1,000 ms and nine colours. Trial counts: L&V 32–40 per condition, Xu 180 per set size; the game's 16 per set size (8 change trials) gives a very noisy K (each set size's K moves in steps of 0.25–1.0). With eight colours and eight squares, every "change" at set size 8 is to a colour already present elsewhere, so it tests colour–location binding rather than colour-set memory; that is legitimate (L&V sampled colours with replacement) but differs from set sizes 2–6.
Change: (a) raise trials to at least 32 per set size in the standard run (or drop set size 2, which adds little to K), keeping the quick option; (b) report the mean of K at 4, 6 and 8 as Xu did (already done), and show the per-size K with its trial count; (c) replace "measured exposure" with a frame-counted estimate or call it a timer estimate; (d) say that practice raises K (Xu Exp. 2: 2.15 → 3.0 over a month).

### V. Brown–Peterson (DecayTest)
Implemented: four digits shown together for 2 s; delays 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 s (two trials each) or 3, 9, 18 s; count back by threes from a random 300–999, typed, auto-submitting after three digits, with a tick every 1.5 s; recall typed; scored by digits in position; "just wait" control.
Against Peterson & Peterson: they used consonant trigrams and numbers precisely because letters and numbers are "categorically dissimilar and hence involve a minimum of interference"; the game uses digits for both the memory items and the counting, which adds similarity-based interference and makes a four-digit number chunkable as a single number. Their count was spoken at 2/s to a metronome; typed counting is roughly 4–6 times slower, so the filler occupies much less of the delay (the "underfilled" flag only fires below one entry per 4 s). They used 3–18 s, eight trials per interval; the game's two trials per delay gives each point only nine possible values. There was no 0-s condition and no "just wait" condition; the rehearsal manipulation in their Exp. 2 was 0–3 s of spoken rehearsal before counting.
Change: (a) use three consonants (the code already defines `CONSONANTS` and `genLetters`, unused) or at least count down with the digits while holding letters; (b) at least four trials per delay in the standard run; (c) push the counting pace (target one entry per 1.5–2 s, tick at 1 s) and flag trials with fewer than one entry per 2 s; (d) keep 0 s only as a ceiling check and say it is not from the original; (e) attribute the "just wait" mode to the lecture, not to Peterson & Peterson.

### VI. Hebb repetition (HebbTest)
Implemented: 24 (or 15) lists at span + 1 (clamped 6–10; 8 if no span); the Hebb list on trials 3, 6, 9 … (8 repetitions in 24 lists; 5 in 15); digits 1/s; recall by position with blanks; fillers share at most one-third of positions with the Hebb list; gain = mean of last two repetitions minus mean of first two, in points; regression slope also computed; awareness question at the end.
Against the literature: nine-digit lists every third trial (Hebb); span + 1 (Norris, Page & Hall); about eight repetitions among ~20 fillers (Page & Norris): the 24-list run matches the standard protocol closely. The 15-list run gives five repetitions, below what the literature uses; label it as a taster. The literature measures the effect as the Hebb slope minus the filler slope (Szmalec; NPH); the game's first-two vs last-two difference is fine for a certificate, but the slope difference is the comparable number and should be the one quoted against "3–4 points per repetition".
Change: (a) headline the slope difference × repetitions alongside the first/last gain; (b) note that fillers gain a little with practice (0.2 points per trial in Szmalec); (c) soften the awareness sentence as in B31; (d) keep "one list every third trial" but drop "24" from the Hebb citation unless verified.

## D. Statements with no support that should be removed or softened

1. "Few people manage more than four or five digits with add-one; three to four with add-three is typical" (Guide II). Only "more than four" for Add-1 is in Kahneman; nothing for Add-3.
2. "Wechsler ... since 1939" as a description of this ladder (lede I). The digit-span task dates from then; this specific procedure does not.
3. "The flash is too brief to name the colours, so this measures the store itself" (lede IV). The control in Luck & Vogel was a verbal load, not brevity.
4. "Cowan's K is where the modern number of about four comes from" (lede IV). One source among several.
5. "Noticing helps a little" (Hebb reading). No source; the literature says awareness does not affect recall gains (with one contrary study).
6. "random lists landing between about 40% and 70%" (Hebb reading). Page & Norris say around 50%.
7. "The 'just wait' control shows ... four digits survive twenty seconds easily" (Guide V). A prediction, not a finding; Peterson & Peterson had no such condition.
8. "Bower & Springston (1970): FBI CIA IBM vs FB ICI AIB M". Not their example as far as any source shows.
9. "the certificate reports the measured exposure" (Guide, README). It reports a JavaScript timer, not a display measurement.
10. "Derek Muller demonstrates it in The Science of Thinking (2017)". Unverified.
11. "Losing the start of a list is unusual; it often means the first digits were never properly taken in, which is an attention story" (span reading). No source.
12. "Scores are piecewise-linear maps of the research bands" (README/Guide). True only for digit span and K; the plus-one, formula and repeat bands are the lab's own.
13. "Hebb (1961): 24 nine-digit lists". The 24 is unverified from anything opened.
14. "Szmalec et al. (2009) ... lists at span + 1, ~8 repetitions". Szmalec used nine fixed-length syllable lists and 12 repetitions.

## E. Better or more primary citations

- **Instrument III (chunking with symbol strings):** Zhilin & Tkachuk (2013), *Int. J. Phys. Chem. Bower & Springston (1970) is the sequential-letters precedent. Egan & Schwartz (1979, *Mem. Cognit.* 7, 149–158) for circuit diagrams and McKeithen et al. (1981, *Cognit. - **Add-1 procedure:** cite Kahneman (2011) *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, ch. 2, for the beat, the two-beat wait and the "few people beyond four digits" line, and Kahneman & Beatty (1966) only for the pupil result. Kahneman (1973) *Attention and Effort* would be the primary book description but I could not open it.
- **SF's consonant span:** Chase & Ericsson (1981), *Skilled memory* (Carnegie-Mellon/ONR report ADA114635), or Ericsson & Chase (1982), *American Scientist* 70, 607–615, for "around 6" and "no transfer".
- **Digit-span norms for a two-trial ladder:** the WMS-III young-adult figures quoted by Woods (forward 6.74, backward 5.07 for ages 20–29) are closer to what the game measures than Woods' staircase Mean Span. For a large modern normative sample, the WAIS-IV manual (not open access) is the primary.
- **Hebb protocol:** Page & Norris (2009) for the standard "eight repetitions among ~20 fillers" and "3–4 points per repetition"; Araya, Oberauer & Saito (2022) for measured gains with 8-item lists; Guérard et al. (2011) and Weitz et al. (2011) for awareness.
- **Peterson curve numbers:** quote Fig. 2 (all latencies) if keeping 80/55/9%, and say so; or quote Fig. 3 (55/40/6%) which is the curve in most textbooks.
- **Browser timing:** Anwyl-Irvine et al. carries the per-OS and per-browser overrun numbers; Bridges et al. is better cited for the macOS one-frame display lag and for the finding that online duration precision is mostly within a frame.
- **Change-detection norms:** Xu et al. (2018) is the right large-sample single-probe reference; add Luck & Vogel (2013) for the "3–4 objects" summary and note that Xu's Exp. 2 practice gains (K 2.15 → 3.0) explain why a second run scores higher.
