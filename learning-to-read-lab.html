An experiment on learning, done on yourself

A is 1, B is 2, C is 3. Can you learn to read again?

All of us learned ABCD so long ago that we don't remember how hard it was. In this lab we learn to read one more time. The letters are written as numbers: A is 1, B is 2, C is 3, and so on up to Z, which is 26. We start with single letters, then we read words, and then sentences, the same way a child does.

The lab times every letter and gives you points. At the end it shows you how quickly you picked up the code, and where you got stuck.

The five levels

  1. One number, one letter. You see 20 and you tap T.
  2. Words, with a gap after each letter. You see 3 · 1 · 20 and you type CAT.
  3. Words, with no gaps. You see 2120. Is it B, A, T or is it U, T?
  4. Sentences. You type them word by word.
  5. Read for the meaning. You read a sentence in your head, and then we ask you what it said.
How long

Come back to this every day

The one-minute sprint

You get one minute. Read as many letters as you can. Your count of right letters is your score, and the lab keeps your best.

Nobody learns to read in twelve minutes. Do one sprint a day for a week, and look at your line on the Results page.

What you read

How to play

  1. Keep your pen and paper away. Don't write the code down. Work out each letter in your head, until you start to remember it.
  2. Tap the letter keys on the screen. On a laptop, type on the keyboard.
  3. A right letter gives you 10 points, and up to 10 more if you are fast. Five right letters in a row make every letter worth one and a half times as much, and ten in a row make it double.
  4. A wrong letter gives nothing, and your row of right letters starts again from zero. So don't guess.

This is not a test of how clever you are. We want to feel, for a few minutes, what a five-year-old faces with a page of print. Everything stays in this browser unless you export it.

How this lab works, and what the research says

Have you played the five levels? If not, play first. You will follow this page much better when you have your own numbers in front of you.

Where does this lab come from?

The number code is from Daniel Willingham's book Why Don't Students Like School? (2009). In chapter 5, on page 86, he prints one sentence in numbers and asks the reader to read it. Under it he writes:

"This sentence is written in a simple code: 1 = A, 2 = B, 3 = C, and so on, with a new line denoting a new word. The efforts of a beginning reader are a bit like your efforts to decode this sentence, because the value of each letter must be figured out."

He also says that if we read it without writing anything down, we "will likely forget the beginning of the sentence by the time you are decoding the end." Level 5 of this lab is that demonstration.

Willingham gives one sentence. This lab adds the levels, the timing and the points, so that we can watch ourselves go from letters to words to sentences. That path is much older than the book. It was first measured in 1899, on telegraph operators.

The telegraph study of 1899

William Bryan and Noble Harter wanted to know how a person learns to receive Morse code. A telegraph operator hears clicks and has to turn them into letters, then words, then messages. It is learning to read by ear.

They tested one student, John Shaw, every week from October 1896 to May 1897. Each week he did three tests: letters that made no words, words that made no sentences, and ordinary sentences. The paper gives a graph and no table, so these numbers are read off the graph and are only roughly right:

What he receivedOctober 1896May 1897
Letters that made no wordsabout 18 to 20 letters a minuteabout 48
Words that made no sentencesabout 18 to 20about 65
Ordinary sentencesabout 18 to 20about 108

At the start, all three were the same. He was catching one letter at a time, and it didn't matter what the letters made. Seven months later he received sentences at more than double his speed for loose letters. The letters were the same clicks. What had changed is that he now heard words and phrases as whole things.

Bryan and Harter wrote that learning the telegraph "consists in acquiring a hierarchy of psycho-physical habits" (a fancy way to say habits built on top of habits): letter habits first, then word habits, then habits for whole phrases. Operators told them the same thing in their own words: "At the outset one 'hustles for the letters.' Later one is 'after words.'"

They also saw that learners get stuck for weeks at one speed. In their earlier paper of 1897 they say most beginners become discouraged at this point. Their reading of it was this:

"He has no useful freedom for higher language units which he has not earned by making the lower ones automatic."

In plain words: we can't pay attention to words while every letter still needs our attention. This is one student and a graph from 1899, so take the numbers lightly. The idea has lasted.

Do we count, or do we know?

When you saw 20 in Level 1, what did you do? Most of us count along the alphabet, or jump to a letter we know and count from there. After some practice we see 20 and T comes by itself. The chart on your result sheet tries to show which one you were doing.

Gordon Logan studied this change in 1988 with a task he called alphabet arithmetic. Students saw a line such as A + 2 = C and had to say if it was true. At the start they counted, and each extra step of counting took them 400 to 500 thousandths of a second. In a later study (Logan and Klapp, 1991) eight students practised for 12 sessions. In the first session each extra step cost 486 thousandths of a second. By the twelfth session it cost 45, and counting no longer showed in the numbers. They were not counting faster. They had stopped counting, because they remembered the answers.

In the same paper, 16 other students simply learned six such facts by heart from flash cards, in at most 15 minutes. For those six facts the counting was gone at once. For facts they had not learned, each step still cost 447 thousandths of a second.

Our Level 1 is not Logan's task. He asked students to add to a letter, and we ask for the letter of a number. So don't compare your thousandths of a second with his. What we take from him is the way to look: if later letters take longer, we are counting, and if the line is flat, we know.

How a child gets from letters to words

Linnea Ehri has studied for many years how children learn to read words. She describes four phases. These are her words, from her 2020 paper:

  1. First, children "rely primarily on visually salient cues and context cues but not letter–sound cues." They know the word on the shop's board from its colours, and not from its letters.
  2. Then they "can use their knowledge of letter names or sounds to read and write but cannot decode unfamiliar words." They catch the first letter and guess the rest.
  3. Then they "have acquired decoding skill and can fully analyze and form grapheme–phoneme connections within words." (A grapheme is a letter or a group of letters that stands for one sound.) This is C, A, T, one sound at a time. It is our Level 2.
  4. Last, they "have acquired knowledge of larger consolidated spelling patterns representing spoken syllables and morphemes." They read in bigger pieces.

She also says what happens to a word a child has read enough times: "readers can read them as whole units quickly and automatically". And she adds: "This applies to all words, not just high-frequency or irregularly spelled words." That is what we were looking for with THE in Level 4.

Why slow reading loses the meaning

In Level 5 many of us reach the end of a sentence and find that the beginning has gone. David LaBerge and S. Jay Samuels wrote the well-known paper on this in 1974. Their idea was that we have only so much attention. If reading the letters takes all of it, nothing is left for the meaning. Samuels wrote it this way in 2006: "If all of the reader's attention is focused on decoding, comprehension cannot occur at the same time."

We could not open the 1974 paper itself, only its summary and Samuels's later article. So this lab gives no numbers from it.

What is the lab's own, and where does it differ from a child?

  • Nobody has published an experiment with this A = 1 code. We looked. Willingham's sentence is a demonstration, not an experiment. The closest study we found is by Taylor, Plunkett and Nation (2011): 16 adults learned to read 36 made-up words written in made-up characters, in one sitting of 30 to 45 minutes. After that they could also read new words in those characters.
  • Level 3, where we decide if 21 is U or B and A, is the lab's own idea. A child has a problem like it in English: S and H are one sound in SHIP and two sounds in MISHAP. A baby has it with speech, because we don't pause between words when we talk. Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) found that 8-month-old babies could pick out the "words" in two minutes of made-up speech with no pauses. We read only the summary of that paper.
  • We already know English. We know the alphabet's order, we know the words, and we know what a sentence is. A five-year-old is learning the shapes, the sounds and many of the words at the same time. So what we feel here is a small part of what the child faces.
  • Twelve minutes is very short. John Shaw took seven months. That is why the sprint is there.

How the lab counts

Points

A right letter gives 10 points. A fast right letter gives up to 10 more: the full 10 if you take one second or less, and nothing extra if you take four seconds or more. After five right letters in a row every letter is worth one and a half times as much, and after ten in a row it is worth double. A wrong letter gives nothing and your row starts again. A sentence understood in Level 5 gives 100 points.

Stars

Each level gives one to three stars. For three stars you need 95 of every 100 letters right at the first try and a fast middle time for a letter (1.5 s in Levels 1 and 2, 2.0 s in Level 3, 1.8 s in Level 4). For two stars you need 85 of every 100 right and 2.5 s, 3.5 s or 3.0 s. In Level 5 you get three stars for every sentence right and two stars for one mistake.

The score out of 100

  • Right letters, 30 marks. The share of all letters in Levels 1 to 4 that you got right at the first try. 70 of every 100 gives no marks and 100 of every 100 gives all 30.
  • Speed at the end, 30 marks. Your middle time for a letter in the sentences of Level 4. Five seconds gives no marks and 1.2 seconds gives all 30.
  • Getting faster, 25 marks. How much faster you were in the second half of Level 1 than in the first half. 35 of every 100 faster gives all 25.
  • Meaning, 15 marks. The share of sentences you understood in Level 5.

All these bands are the lab's own. No study gives them. We picked them so that a first run by a college student lands somewhere in the middle, and we will change them when we have numbers from a real class. If you knew the code before you started, "getting faster" will be low, because you had little left to learn.

Time for a letter

For Levels 1 to 4 it is the middle one (the median) of your times for the letters you got right at the first try. A time is left out if you had moved to another tab. On a phone you also have to find the key, so phone times are a little longer than laptop times.

What does this mean for how we study?

  • When a page of a new subject looks hard, ask yourself first: "Am I stuck on the idea, or am I still reading the symbols one by one?" Very often it is the symbols.
  • Practise the small things until you don't have to think about them: the tables, the standard formulas, the syntax of a loop. Bryan and Harter's operator could only listen for words after the letters had become automatic.
  • Being right is not the end. In Level 1 you were probably right from the first minute, and still slow. Keep practising after you are right, until you are right without effort.
  • Do it on many days. Look at your sprint line after a week, and compare it with the first day.
  • When you stop getting faster for a while, don't decide "I'm bad at this." Bryan and Harter saw the same flat stretch in their learners, and after it the speed went up again.
