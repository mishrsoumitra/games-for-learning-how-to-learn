Lecture 4 · Attention · four instruments

How long can you keep your attention on one thing?

Attention span is not one number, so this lab measures it four ways: how long you can keep a count going, how quickly you notice that your mind has drifted, how often a lapse slips past you, and how steady your reactions stay as the minutes pass. Before you start, guess how many minutes you can watch a second hand without drifting. Write the number down.

How to run it

Instrument II · Press for every digit except 3

Press for every digit except 3.

This is the Sustained Attention to Response Task (Robertson and others, 1997). Digits 1 to 9 appear briefly, one every 1.15 seconds, in random sizes. Press for all of them except 3. A press on the 3 means your hand kept going while your mind was elsewhere.

Length
Thought probes

How to run it

  1. Press Space (or tap the circle) as soon as each digit appears.
  2. Do not press when the digit is 3.
  3. Press quickly, but do not let speed cost you the 3. Getting the 3 right matters more. Most people press on the 3 far more than they expect.
  4. A few times, the task pauses to ask what you were just thinking about. Answer truthfully. There is no wrong answer.

Instrument III · Stop the counter

Stop the counter as fast as you can.

This is the Psychomotor Vigilance Task, first built by Dinges and Powell (1985), in the three-minute form of Basner, Mollicone and Dinges (2011). After a random wait of one to four seconds, a millisecond counter starts running. Stop it. A reaction slower than 355 ms counts as a lapse. That is the rule used in sleep research.

Guide

What attention span means, and how to read your numbers

Attention span means three different things

When people say their attention span is short, they usually mean one of three measurable things. This lab pulls them apart on purpose.

  • Sustained attention (vigilance): how well you keep watching a dull stream for a rare event, and how much that ability decays with time on task. Norman Mackworth measured it in 1948 with a clock whose pointer stepped round the dial and now and then jumped two steps at once. Detection fell over the first half hour and stayed low for two hours. The skip-watch mode of the clock is a short version of his test with far more frequent signals, so it shows you the task rather than reproducing his result.
  • Lapses: brief moments where the mind is simply not there. The digit task (SART) catches them as presses on the forbidden 3; the reaction counter (PVT) catches them as responses slower than 355 ms. Healthy adults on the standard 225-digit version press on the 3 about a quarter of the time (Manly and others, 2000). Undergraduates who rush press on it nearly half the time (Seli and others, 2013).
  • Mind-wandering, with or without awareness: researchers separate tune-outs (you know your mind has drifted) from zone-outs (you only realise when something interrupts you) (Smallwood, McSpadden and Schooler, 2007). The drift key measures the first kind; checkpoints and thought probes catch the second. Probe studies in real lectures report anything from about 15% to 45% of probes, depending on the class and the question asked. Some studies find more wandering later in the hour (Risko and others, 2012). The largest one, across a whole semester, did not (Wammes and others, 2016).

How the clock scores you

Your headline figure is the typical focus stretch: the median time between a fresh start and the moment you pressed the drift key. If you never pressed it, the figure is the whole run, marked "≥", and the checkpoints decide whether that was real focus or an unnoticed drift.

A checkpoint counts as tracking when your count lands within 5% (or 3 seconds) of the clock, slipping within 15%, and lost beyond that. Because a drift restarts the count, any lost checkpoint is a lapse you did not catch yourself.

Count-and-checkpoint is not a published test. Its parts are. The drift key comes from research on self-caught versus probe-caught mind-wandering (Smallwood and Schooler, 2006; Schooler and others, 2011). Counting with a "lost count" key comes from the Breath Counting Task (Levinson and others, 2014) and the Metronome Counting Task (Anderson and Farb, 2020). Nobody has published a distribution of focus stretches on a second hand, so the bands below are this lab's own, not research norms.

under 45 sShort stretches. Very common when tired, hungry or anxious. Try again after sleep before drawing conclusions.
45 s – 2 minBrief stretches. Typical for a dull task with nothing to hold on to. Noticing quickly is the skill to build.
2 – 5 minSolid stretches on a task designed to be boring. Real study material should hold you longer than this.
over 5 minLong stretches. Check that the checkpoints agree.

Watching a second hand is meant to be dull. Use it as a floor. Interesting material, a clear goal and a little novelty all stretch attention well beyond what you see here.

Reading the other three instruments

DigitsPresses on the 3: healthy adults average about 25% on this 225-digit version, with a wide spread (Manly and others, 2000). Under 12% is sharp; 12 to 40% is typical; above 40% usually means you were pressing too fast to stop in time. Errors are preceded by speeding up (Robertson and others, 1997). Some researchers read that as autopilot; others as a choice to go fast (Dang and others, 2018). Either way, slow down a little and the 3s come back.
ReactionRested adults on laboratory hardware respond about four times a second (about 250 ms), with a median of one lapse over 355 ms in three minutes (Antler and others, 2022). A browser adds roughly 70 to 100 ms to every reaction (Anwyl-Irvine and others, 2021), so expect slower speeds and more "lapses" here. Compare runs with your own earlier runs. Lapses climb steeply after a short night.
RhythmJitter is the spread of your tap-to-beat offsets. In Seli's undergraduates, worked back from their published figures, the spread over five taps was roughly 55 ms while on task and 65 ms just before a report of wandering, on a laboratory keyboard. Here, under about 60 ms is steady, 60 to 100 wavering, above 100 erratic, and these cut-offs are this lab's own. The comparison that matters is the one the research makes: your jitter before probes where you reported wandering, against probes where you were on task.

How the score is computed

Every run also gets one figure from 0 to 100, so that a trend can be drawn. The bands are this lab's own, informed by the research where norms exist (the SART error range and PVT lapse counts) and chosen by hand where they do not. Compare the score only with your own earlier runs on the same instrument. The instruments measure different things and their scores are not interchangeable.

Clock · count40% from your typical focus stretch (45 s ≈ 35, 2 min ≈ 60, 5 min ≈ 85, 10 min = 100) and 60% from the checkpoints (tracking counts in full, slipping half, lost nothing). Five points off each time you left the tab.
Clock · skipsThe percentage of skips you caught, minus four points per false alarm.
DigitsThe share of 3s you did not press on, multiplied by the share of other digits you did press for. Never pressing scores nothing, and so does pressing on every 3.
Reaction60% from the lapse rate (none = 100, one trial in twenty ≈ 85, three in twenty ≈ 60, three in ten ≈ 30, half = 0) and 40% from your response speed, the mean of 1 divided by each reaction time (3.8 per second = 100, 3.2 ≈ 80, 2.7 ≈ 55, 2.1 ≈ 25). Three points off per false start. Speed and lapses are the two measures sleep research finds most sensitive (Basner and Dinges, 2011).
RhythmFrom timing jitter (30 ms = 100, 60 ms = 75, 100 ms = 45, 150 ms = 15), scaled down by the share of beats you missed, minus a point per extra tap.

Stopping early. Press Esc or Stop at any moment; that also leaves full screen. If you have done enough for a measurement (about 20 seconds of the clock, 18 scored digits, 5 reactions or 15 beats) the run is scored from what you did and marked provisional when at least half the run was done (it counts half in your average) or premature when less than half was (it counts a quarter). Shorter than that, nothing is saved. Attention usually slips later in a run, so a partial run tends to flatter you. The complete run is the fair comparison.

Common claims, checked against the research

  • "Humans now have an eight-second attention span, less than a goldfish." Made up. The figure came from a 2015 marketing report that cited a website which could produce no research behind it (Maybin, BBC, 2017; Bradbury, 2016).
  • "Average attention on a screen is 47 seconds." Real, but it measures something else: how long knowledge workers stay on one screen before switching, logged by Gloria Mark's team (2.5 minutes in 2004, 75 seconds in 2012, 47 in recent years). It is a habit of switching, not a limit of the brain.
  • "Students can only pay attention for 10 to 15 minutes of a lecture." Repeated in teaching guides for decades, but reviews found no study behind it (Wilson and Korn, 2007; Bradbury, 2016). When chemistry students reported lapses with clickers, the first came as early as 30 seconds in, then again around 4 to 5 minutes, with short lapses far outnumbering long ones and fewer of them in the segments after demonstrations and clicker questions (Bunce, Flens and Neiles, 2010).

How to use this lab well

  1. Take a rested baseline first, at the same time of day you plan to repeat. Sleep, caffeine and time of day move every one of these numbers.
  2. Repeat, and compare yourself with yourself. A single run is noisy. A trend across two weeks is real.
  3. Treat pressing the drift key as a win. The goal is not zero drifts. It is noticing sooner.
  4. Try the clock before and after a change you are curious about: a walk, a phone in another room, ten minutes of breathing.
  5. On the digits, do not rush. Getting the 3 right matters more than speed, and the SART only measures lapses if you are trying to be accurate (Seli, Cheyne and Smilek, 2012).
  6. None of this is a diagnosis. Attention tests are used in clinics alongside interviews and history. A browser page on its own cannot screen for anything.
